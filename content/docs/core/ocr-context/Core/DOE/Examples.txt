# The Goal of Industrial Experimentation

In any manufacturing process, the primary interest is exploring the relationship between Key Input Process Variables (Factors) and Output Performance Characteristics (Response). The objective is to identify the "vital few" variables from the "trivial many".

## Control Factors (Inputs)

- Cutting Speed  
- Feed Rate  
- Coolant Type  
- Depth of Cut  

**[Image Description]**: Inputs (factors) flowing into a system producing output (response)

## Response (Output)

- Surface Finish  

## Key Concept

### Engineering Objective

Optimize the setting of the Control Factors to achieve the target Surface Finish with minimal variation.

# The Intuitive Trap: One-Variable-At-A-Time (OVAT)

## The Intuition

It feels logical to isolate a single cause by keeping all variables constant and moving one lever. This is the standard engineering approach.

## The Reality

OVAT depends on guesswork, luck, and intuition. It is resource-heavy and inefficient.

“OVAT... may yield false optimum conditions for the process.”

# The Fatal Flaw: Missing the Interaction Effect

A Case Study in Temperature vs. Pressure

# Enter Design of Experiments (DOE)

A statistical approach where deliberate changes are made to input variables simultaneously to observe and identify the reasons for changes in the output response.

1920s - Sir Ronald Fisher  
Rothamsted Agricultural Research. Separating fertilizer effects from soil conditions.  

Mid-Century - Plackett & Burman / Box  
Industrial application in chemical industries.  

Modern Era - Taguchi & Shainin  
Robust Design in Manufacturing. Focusing on variability reduction.

# Principle 1: Randomization

Insurance against external noise.

Standard Order (Non-Randomized)

**[Image Description]**: Sequential runs (A A A B B B) affected by rising ambient temperature → biased result

Randomized Order (DOE Standard)

**[Image Description]**: Mixed sequence (A B B A B A) averaging out noise → valid conclusion

Why Randomize?  
To neutralize extraneous factors like humidity, machine warm-up, operator fatigue.

“Failure to randomize... mitigates the statistical validity of an experiment.”

---

# Principle 2: Replication vs. Repetition

Doing it twice vs. Measuring it twice.

## Replication

**[Image Description]**: Operator resetting machine and rerunning full experiment

Captures SETUP variability + Measurement variability  

Estimates Experimental Error  

## Repetition

**[Image Description]**: Same part measured repeatedly with same instrument

Captures ONLY Measurement variability  

Does not estimate Experimental Error  

Without Replication, we cannot distinguish a true effect from background noise.

---

# Principle 3: Blocking

Handling Nuisance Factors.

Is there a Nuisance Factor? (e.g. Batches, Shifts)

- No → Standard Randomization  
- Unknown → RANDOMIZE (spread risk)  
- Known → BLOCK (group homogeneous units)  

Comparisons are made within blocks to eliminate nuisance variation from error.

# Degrees of Freedom (DoF)

The Currency of Experimentation

\[
\mathrm{DoF} = \mathrm{N} - 1
\]

- Number of independent comparisons  
- Each factor consumes DoF  
- Must reserve DoF to estimate error  

**[Image Description]**: Individuals connected showing limited comparisons (N-1 concept)

# Measurement System Analysis (MSA)

"If you can't measure it, you can't improve it."

\[
\sigma^2(\text{Total}) = \sigma^2(\text{Product}) + \sigma^2(\text{Measurement})
\]

# Assessing Gauge R&R

**[Image Description]**: Measurement error split into Repeatability (equipment variation) and Reproducibility (operator variation)

## Precision-to-Tolerance Ratio

\[
P/T = \frac{6 \times \text{Measurement Error}}{\text{Tolerance}}
\]

- < 10%: Acceptable  
- 10%–30%: Conditional  
- > 30%: Unacceptable
