# Factors, Levels &amp; Response Variables

A successful experiment begins by clearly defining the inputs (factors) to vary and the outputs (responses) to measure, treating the process as a system.

## Factors (Inputs / X's)

Controllable: Parameters you change (e.g., Temp, Speed). Noise (Uncontrollable): External variables (e.g., Humidity, Batch). Can be Qualitative (Type A/B) or Quantitative (Values).

## Levels

The specific settings or values chosen for each factor in the experiment (e.g., Low (-1) and High (+1)).

## Responses (Outputs / Y's)

Critical to Quality (CTQ) characteristics measured to evaluate performance (e.g., Yield, Surface Finish, Defect Rate).

- Ideally, choose responses that are continuous (measurable) rather than binary (pass/fail) for better statistical power.
- Adjustable
- Hard to Control
- Measured Goal

# Practical Constraints &amp; Risks

## Common Industrial Constraints

### Time &amp; Cost Constraints
Experiments consume production time and raw materials.
**Mitigation:** Use fractional designs or screening to reduce run count.

### Downtime Windows
Experiments may require stopping regular production.
**Mitigation:** Plan experiments during maintenance shifts or low-demand periods.

### Material/Batch Variability
Inconsistent raw materials can mask factor effects.
**Mitigation:** Use blocking techniques to account for batch-to-batch variation.

### Operator Availability &amp; Skill
Shift changes and skill gaps introduce noise.
**Mitigation:** Standardize SOPs (Standard Operating Procedures) before starting.

## Strategic Risk Management

### Ethics &amp; Safety First
Some factor levels may create unsafe conditions or unethical outcomes (e.g., poor quality products reaching customers).
Review safety limits for all factors.
Isolate experimental batches from shipping.

### Digital Mitigation
Before physical runs, use simulation or pilot tests to reduce risk.

# Pitfalls of One-Factor-at-a-Time (OFAT)

- **Misses Interactions**
OFAT assumes factors act independently. It fails to detect when the effect of one factor depends on the level of another (e.g., temperature only helps if pressure is high).

- **Low Efficiency**
Requires significantly more runs to achieve the same precision as a factorial design. DoE provides "more bang for the buck."

- **Limited Coverage**
Explores only a tiny fraction of the process space (the axes), leaving the corners and center—where optimal settings often hide—untested.

- **Factorial Designs (DoE)** solve these issues by varying multiple factors simultaneously.

# Sampling &amp; Measurement Systems (MSA)

Before analyzing experimental data, we must validate the data collection process. If the "ruler" is broken, the data is meaningless ("Garbage In, Garbage Out").

## Sampling Strategy

Representativeness: Random sampling prevents bias. Sample Size (n): Larger samples reduce standard error (\$\\sigma\\sqrt{n}\$) and increase power.

## Accuracy vs. Precision

Accuracy (Bias): Difference between average measurement and true value. Precision (Variation): Spread of repeated measurements (Standard Deviation).

## Gage R&amp;R (Precision Components)

Repeatability: Variation from the gage (same part, same operator). Reproducibility: Variation from the operators (same part, different operators).

# Uncertainty: Estimates &amp; Intervals

"A single number is virtually guaranteed to be wrong. We must quantify how wrong we might be."

- Point Estimate (Best Guess)
A single value (e.g., $\hat{x} = 24.5$) derived from sample data. Precise, but gives no indication of reliability or risk.

- Confidence Interval (CI)
A range for the True Mean ($\mu$). "We are 95% confident the process average is between 24.1 and 24.9."

- Prediction Interval (PI)
A range for a Single Future Value. Always wider than CI. "The next unit produced will fall between 22.0 and 27.0."

▲ In industrial settings, Prediction Intervals are often more relevant for quality control than Confidence Intervals.

ANALYSIS PHASE

# Hypothesis Testing

A structured framework to distinguish signal from noise. In DoE, it determines if factor effects are real or due to random chance.

## Practical Insight

Statistical Significance ≠ Practical Importance. A tiny effect can be "significant" with enough data, but useless for the business. Always check the effect size.

## KEY METRICS

- P-value: Probability of seeing the data if H0 is true.
- s: Alpha: Risk tolerance for False Positives (Type I).
- β: Beta: Risk of missing a real effect (Type II).

## 1. Formulate Hypotheses

Null (H0): Factor has NO effect (= 0).
Alternative (H1): Factor has an effect (# 0).

## 2. The Decision Rule (Compare P vs α)

**T A C H O N E C I C D**
REJECT H0
"Statistically Significant"

P-value ≥ 0.05
FAIL TO REJECT H0
"No Evidence of Effect"

## 3. Decision Risks (Errors)

|   | Truth: H0 is True (No Effect) | Truth: H0 is False (Real Effect)  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  Reject H0 | Type I Error (α) False Positive | Correct Decision Power (1-β)  |
|  Fail to Reject | Correct Decision True Negative | Type II Error (β) False Negative  |

## 4. Final Verdict

Is the effect Significant (statistically) AND Substantial (practically)? Action: Implement change only if both conditions are met.

# Why Randomize? The First Defense Against Bias

Randomization is the "insurance policy" of experimental design. It protects your results from unknown or uncontrollable factors that change over time.

## Neutralize Time Trends

Prevents confusion between factor effects and time-dependent variables like tool wear, machine warm-up, or chemical degradation.

## Mitigate Operator Effects

Balances out fatigue, shift changes, or learning curve effects that typically occur during an experimental sequence.

## Validate Statistics

Ensures the "independence of errors" assumption required for ANOVA and t-tests is valid.

# Replication &amp; Precision

"Replication is the independent repetition of each factor combination run, essential for estimating experimental error."

## Estimate Pure Error

Allows calculation of the true experimental error variance ($\backslash\sigma^{2}$) separate from lack-of-fit, enabling valid significance tests (F-tests).

## Stabilize Estimates

Reduces the standard error of the mean ($\sigma / n \vee$). More replicates = narrower confidence intervals and higher power to detect effects.

## Replication vs. Repetition

Replication: Full setup reset (captures setup error). Repetition: Multiple measures of same setup (only captures measurement error).

# Blocking to Handle Nuisance Factors

Blocking is a technique used to eliminate the effect of known "nuisance" variables that influence the response but are not the primary interest of the experiment.

## The Golden Rule

"Block what you can, randomize what you cannot."

- **Increases Precision**
Removes block-to-block variation from the experimental error (MSE).

- **Isolates Noise**
Prevents nuisance factors (e.g., shifts, batches) from obscuring real effects.

# Factorial Thinking vs. OFAT Approach

Traditional One-Factor-at-a-Time (OFAT) methods often fail to capture system complexity. Factorial designs change varying factors simultaneously to maximize information density.

- **Interaction Detection**
OFAT holds factors constant, blinding you to interactions. Factorial design reveals how factors influence each other.

- **Statistical Efficiency**
In factorial designs, every data point contributes to estimating every effect, providing higher precision per run.

- **Broader Validity**
Conclusions are valid over a range of experimental conditions (the "design space") rather than a single point.

# Estimating Effects &amp; The Pareto Plot

## Calculating Main Effects

An effect is the change in the average response when a factor moves from its Low (-1) to High (+1) level.

Effect = Average(Y at High) - Average(Y at Low)

## Sparsity of Effects

In most industrial experiments, the system is dominated by a few "vital" main effects and low-order interactions. The majority are "trivial many" noise.

## Interpreting the Plot

Magnitude: Length of bar indicates strength of influence.

Significance: Effects exceeding the critical t-value limit (red line) are statistically significant.

Prioritization: Focus process improvement efforts on the top factors.

Use the Pareto plot to screen out non-active factors early.

KEY INTERACTIONS &amp; METHODOLOGY

# Interaction Plots and Interpretation

An interaction exists when the effect of one factor on the response depends on the level of another factor. The interaction plot is the primary tool for visualizing this dependency.

## The "Parallelism" Rule

Parallel Lines: Factors are independent (No Interaction). Non-Parallel Lines: Interaction exists. The greater the divergence, the stronger the interaction.

## Interaction Types

Synergistic: Factors reinforce each other (response increases more than sum of parts).
Antagonistic: Factors hinder each other (lines cross or converge strongly).

## Interpretation Trap

Do not interpret Main Effects in isolation if significant interactions are present. The "average" effect may be misleading.

Determine the full factorial setup.

### 2. Select Confounding Effect

Choose the highest order interaction to sacrifice.

Select ABC. Information on ABC interaction will be lost (confounded with block effect).

### 3. Apply Generator Rule

Assign runs based on the sign of the interaction.

Block 1: ABC = - (Negative)

# Degrees of Freedom in Sample Variance

## Logic Flow

### 1 Define "Freedom to Vary"

Start with n independent data points. Before any calculation, you have n degrees of freedom.

Data Set: $\{\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,\dots ,\mathbf{x}_n\}$

Initial df = n

### 2 Introduce Constraint

To calculate variance $(s^2)$, we must first calculate the sample mean $(\overline{x})$.

Constraint: The sum of deviations from the mean must equal zero. $\Sigma (x_i - \overline{x}) = 0$

### 3 Determine Final DoF

One value is no longer free to vary; it is determined by the others to satisfy the constraint.

$$
\text{FAVE } \hat{O} \text{ AÉ}
$$

## Numerical Example (n=5)

Mean $(\overline{x}) = 30 / 5 = 6$

Degrees of Freedom (DoF) represent the amount of independent information available to estimate effects and variability. In factorial designs, we "spend" this currency to buy knowledge about factors.

# Saturated vs Over-Specified Models

Understanding the critical balance between model complexity and available data. A saturated model uses all degrees of freedom for estimation, leaving none for error assessment.

## Saturated Model (0 Error DF)

Number of parameters = Number of unique runs. Perfect fit to data (R² = 100%), but cannot test significance or estimate experimental error.

## Balanced/Parsimonious Model

Includes only significant terms. Retains sufficient degrees of freedom for error (Residual DF &gt; 0) to validate the model and perform hypothesis tests.

## Remediation Strategies

1. Replicate runs to add pure error DF.
2. Sparsity of Effects: Assume higher-order interactions are negligible and pool them into error.

## DF Balance Gauge

## WARNING

A saturated model fits noise as well as signal. Without error DF, you are "flying blind" regarding the true significance of your factors.

Tip: Aim for at least 3-5 degrees of freedom for error to get a reliable estimate of noise.

STATISTICAL PRINCIPLES

# Power &amp; Precision vs. Degrees of Freedom

## Tighter Confidence Intervals

Confidence Intervals (CI) depend on the t-statistic. With few DoF, the t-distribution has "fat tails," resulting in large multiplier values and wide, uninformative intervals.

Result: More DoF → Smaller t-value → Narrower CI (Higher Precision).

## Higher Statistical Power

Power is the probability of detecting a significant effect when one truly exists. Low Error DoF makes it harder to distinguish signal from noise, increasing Type II errors (missed discoveries).

The Trade-off: Increasing DoF usually requires more experimental runs (replication), which increases cost. Aim for at least 10-15 Error DoF for a robust design.

# Lack-of-Fit Analysis &amp; Center Points

Adding center points to a factorial design allows for an independent estimate of pure error and provides a check for non-linear (curvature) effects in the response surface.

- **Center Points (0,0)**
Experimental runs set at the midpoint of all factor levels. They do not alter the orthogonality of the design but provide degrees of freedom for pure error.

- **Detecting Curvature**
By comparing the average response at the center ($\bar{y}_C$) with the average of factorial points ($\bar{y}_F$), we test for quadratic effects: $H_0: \bar{y}_F - \bar{y}_C = 0$.

- **Error Partitioning**
Replication allows decomposition of Residual Sum of Squares ($SS_E$) into:
```
SS Pure Error (Replicates)
SS Lack-of-Fit (Model Bias)
```

KEY FORMULA:
```
SS Residual = SS Pure Error + SS Lack-of-Fit
```

## Interpretation Rule
If the P-value for Curvature is small (typically &lt; 0.05), the linear model is inadequate. A quadratic term (or response surface design like Central Composite) is needed.
