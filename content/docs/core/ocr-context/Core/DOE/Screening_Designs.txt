# What is Screening Design?

Q

## Definition

A screening design is an experimental strategy that efficiently identifies significant factors from a large pool of potential variables by testing a carefully selected subset of all possible combinations.

Q

## Core Principle

Separates the "vital few" factors that significantly impact the response from the "trivial many" that have negligible effects.

Q

## Efficiency Gain

Reduces experimental runs dramatically while maintaining statistical validity. A 7-factor screening may need only 8 runs vs. 128 runs for full factorial.

## Screening vs Full Factorial

### Screening DOE

✓ Tests subset of combinations  
✓ Identifies main effects  
✓ Minimal runs required  
✗ Limited interaction info

### Full Factorial

✓ Tests ALL combinations  
✓ Main + all interactions  
✓ Maximum information  
✗ Exponential run growth

# When to Use Screening Designs

## 1 Large Number of Factors

When investigating 7+ potential factors where full factorial becomes impractical due to exponential run requirements.

## 2 Limited Resources

Budget, time, or material constraints make comprehensive testing infeasible. Screening maximizes information per resource unit.

## 3 Initial Exploration Phase

Early-stage research where factor importance is unknown. Screening identifies which variables warrant deeper investigation.

## 4 Preparation for Optimization

Before conducting response surface methodology (RSM), screening narrows focus to significant factors for optimization studies.

# Types of Screening Designs

## Fractional Factorial Factorial

A carefully selected fraction of full factorial runs that estimates main effects while sacrificing some interaction information.

- Runs: 2' (k-p)
- Resolution III-V
- Some interactions estimable

## Plackett-Burman Burman

Highly economical designs where N is a multiple of 4, allowing up to N-1 factors to be screened in N runs.

- Runs: 8, 12, 16, 20, 24...
- Resolution III
- Main effects only

## Definitive Screening Screening

Modern designs that can estimate main effects, quadratic effects, and two-factor interactions without follow-up runs.

- Runs: 2m+1 or 2m+3
- Three levels per factor
- Quadratic effects included

# Introduction to Plackett-Burman Designs

## Historical Background

Developed by Robin L. Plackett and J. Peter Burman in 1946 while working at the University of Liverpool. Published in the journal Biometrika.

## Mathematical Definition

PB designs are two-level fractional factorial designs where the number of runs N is a multiple of 4 (N = 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24...). Can study up to k = N-1 factors in N experimental runs.

## Key Characteristics

- Each factor at two levels: High (+1) and Low (-1)
- Resolution III design
- Main effects not confounded with each other
- Main effects aliased with 2-factor interactions

## Common PB Design Sizes


| 8-Run Design                                                | 7 Factors  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Screens 7 factors in 8 runs vs. 128 runs for full factorial | &nbsp;     |
| 12-Run Design                                               | 11 Factors |
| Most popular PB design; screens 11 factors in 12 runs       | &nbsp;     |
| 16-Run Design                                               | 15 Factors |
| Screens 15 factors in 16 runs vs. 32,768 full factorial     | &nbsp;     |
| 20-Run Design                                               | 19 Factors |
| Screens 19 factors in 20 runs vs. 524,288 full factorial    | &nbsp;     |

# Constructing Plackett-Burman Designs

## Cyclical Construction Method

### 1. Define First Row

Start with a specific pattern of +1 and -1 values for the first row. This pattern is derived from design generators.

### 2. Cyclic Shift

Each subsequent row is created by shifting the previous row one position to the right, wrapping the last element to the first position.

### 3. Complete with All-Minus Row

The final row (N-th row) consists entirely of -1 values to complete the orthogonal array.

Chapter 02

# Advantages and Limitations of PB Designs

## Advantages

- **Highly Economical**  
Screens k factors in k+1 runs—maximum efficiency for resource-constrained experiments
- **Efficient for Many Factors**  
Ideal when investigating 7-23 factors where full factorial is impractical
- **Orthogonally Balanced**  
Each factor tested equally at high and low levels; main effects estimated independently
- **Easy to Analyze**  
Simple calculations using contrast method; compatible with standard statistical software

## Limitations

- ☐ **No Interaction Estimation**  
Cannot estimate two-factor or higher-order interactions; assumes they are negligible
- ☐ **Resolution III Confounding**  
Main effects are aliased with two-factor interactions; risk of misinterpretation if interactions exist
- ☐ **Limited Design Sizes**  
Only available for N = 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40... (multiples of 4)
- ☐ **No Error Degrees of Freedom**  
When k = N-1 (saturated design), no degrees of freedom left to estimate pure error

# Geometric Designs

## Definition

Geometric designs are experimental designs where the number of runs N is a power of 2: N = 2^4k where k is an integer.

## Common Geometric Design Sizes


| 4       | 8       |
| ------- | ------- |
| 2² runs | 2³ runs |
| 16      | 32      |
| 2⁴ runs | 2⁵ runs |


## Geometric Representation

Can be visualized as vertices of a hypercube in k-dimensional space. Each vertex represents one experimental run.

## 2³ Factorial as Geometric Cube

![img-0.jpeg](img-0.jpeg)

8 vertices = 8 experimental runs; 3 dimensions = 3 factors (A, B, C)

# Non-Geometric Designs

## Definition

Non-geometric designs are experimental designs where N is a multiple of 4 but NOT a power of 2.

## Common Non-Geometric Sizes


| 12         | 20         | 24         |
| ---------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| 11 factors | 19 factors | 23 factors |
| 28         | 36         | 40         |
| 27 factors | 35 factors | 39 factors |


## Alternative Names

- **Hadamard Matrix Designs**: Based on Hadamard matrices
- **Plackett-Burman Designs**: Most common non-geometric type

## Key Characteristics

- **Cannot be Represented as Cubes**  
Unlike geometric designs, non-geometric designs don't correspond to hypercube vertices
- **Cyclical Construction**  
Built using cyclical shifting patterns rather than factorial combinations
- **Same Orthogonality**  
Maintain orthogonal properties for independent main effect estimation

## When to Use Non-Geometric

- → When number of factors falls between geometric sizes (e.g., 11 factors)
- → Maximum economy is critical
- → Only main effects are of interest
- → Follow-up experiments will study interactions

# Introduction to Full Factorial Designs

![img-1.jpeg](img-1.jpeg)

## Definition

A full factorial design is an experimental design that includes all possible combinations of factor levels. For k factors each at 2 levels, requires 2^k experimental runs.

## Complete Information

- ☑ All main effects estimable
- ☑ All two-factor interactions estimable
- ☑ All higher-order interactions estimable
- ☑ No confounding of effects

## When to Use Full Factorial

- → Number of factors is small (≤ 4)
- → Interactions are suspected to be important
- → Adequate resources available
- → Optimization is the goal

## Full Factorial Run Calculation

### 2^k

Total Experimental Runs


| k = 2 factors | 4 runs  |
| ------------- | ------- |
| k = 3 factors | 8 runs  |
| k = 4 factors | 16 runs |
| k = 5 factors | 32 runs |
| k = 6 factors | 64 runs |


## Exponential Growth:

Each additional factor doubles the number of required runs. This rapid growth makes full factorial designs impractical for many real-world scenarios with numerous factors.

Yates Notation: (1) = both low; a = A high; b = B high; ab = both high

## Effect Calculations

### Main Effect A

Effect of factor A averaged over B

$$  
A = \left[ (a + ab) - ((1) + b) \right] / 2  
$$

$$  
A = \left[ (67.5 + 65) - (52 + 61) \right] / 2 = 9.75  
$$

### Main Effect B

Effect of factor B averaged over A

$$  
B = \left[ (b + ab) - ((1) + a) \right] / 2  
$$

$$  
B = \left[ (61 + 65) - (52 + 67.5) \right] / 2 = 3.25  
$$

### AB Interaction

Difference in A effect at B+ vs B−

$$  
AB = [ab - b - a + (1)] / 2  
$$

$$  
AB = [65 - 61 - 67.5 + 52] / 2 = -5.75  
$$

# Understanding Design Resolution

## Definition

Design resolution indicates how clearly effects can be separated in a fractional factorial design. Higher resolution = lower confounding = better design quality.

## Resolution Interpretation Formula

For a design of Resolution R:

→ Main effects (order 1) are confounded with interactions of order R-1  
→ Two-factor interactions (order 2) are confounded with interactions of order R-2

### Example: Resolution IV Design

Main effects confounded with 3-factor interactions (IV - 1 = III). Two-factor interactions confounded with each other (IV - 2 = II).

## Resolution III

☑ Main effects not confounded with each other  
✗ Main effects aliased with 2-factor interactions  
✗ 2-factor interactions aliased with main effects

Use: Initial screening when interactions are negligible

## Resolution IV

☑ Main effects not confounded with each other  
☑ Main effects not aliased with 2-factor interactions  
✗ 2-factor interactions aliased with each other

Use: Characterization when main effects are critical

## Resolution V

☑ Main effects not confounded with each other  
☑ 2-factor interactions not confounded with each other  
☑ Main effects not aliased with 2-factor or 3-factor interactions

Use: Optimization when interactions are important

Chapter 05

# Confounding and Aliasing

## Definition

Confounding (or aliasing) occurs when two or more effects are mathematically intertwined and cannot be estimated separately. The estimated effect is actually a combination of multiple true effects.

## Why Confounding Occurs

1. **Fractional Factorial Designs**  
Testing only a subset of full factorial runs creates mathematical relationships between effects
2. **Design Generators**  
The way additional factors are assigned to interaction columns determines confounding patterns
3. **Trade-off for Efficiency**  
Confounding is the price paid for reducing experimental runs

## Confounding Examples

### Resolution III Example

In a 2'(7-4) fractional factorial (Resolution III):

$$  
A \rightarrow A + BC + DF + EG  
$$

$$  
B \rightarrow B + AC + DG + EF  
$$

Main effect A is confounded with interactions BC, DF, EG

### Resolution IV Example

In a 2'(6-2) fractional factorial (Resolution IV):

$$  
A \rightarrow A + BCDE  
$$

$$  
AB \rightarrow AB + CDE  
$$

Main effects clear of 2-factor interactions; 2-factor interactions confounded with each other

### Resolution V Example

In a 2'(5-1) fractional factorial (Resolution V):

$$  
A \rightarrow A + BCDE  
$$

$$  
AB \rightarrow AB + CDE  
$$

Main effects and 2-factor interactions clear of each other

# Yates Algorithm for Effect Calculation

**What is Yates Algorithm?**

A systematic computational method developed by Frank Yates for calculating factorial effects in 2^k designs. Efficient and programmable.

## Step-by-Step Procedure

1. **List Treatment Combinations**  
Write all 2^k combinations in Yates standard order
2. **Enter Response Totals**  
Record total response for each combination
3. **Create Stage Columns**  
For each of k stages: add pairs (top half), subtract pairs (bottom half)
4. **Final Column = Effect Totals**  
Last column contains contrast totals for each effect
5. **Calculate Effects**  
Effect = Contrast / (2^(k-1) * n)

## Yates Algorithm: 2³ Example


| Treatment | Response | Stage 1 | Stage 2 | Stage 3 | Effect |
| --------- | -------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------ |
| (1)       | 52       | 119.5   | 245.5   | 529.9   | Mean   |
| a         | 67.5     | 126     | 284     | 13.5    | A      |
| b         | 61       | 148     | 19.5    | -5.5    | B      |
| ab        | 65       | 136     | -6      | -25.5   | AB     |
| c         | 72       | 15.5    | 6.5     | 38.5    | C      |
| ac        | 76       | 4       | -12     | -25.5   | AC     |
| bc        | 73       | 4       | -11.5   | -18.5   | BC     |
| abc       | 63       | -10     | -14     | -2.5    | ABC    |


**Interpretation:** Factor C has the largest effect (38.5), followed by A (13.5). AB interaction is significant (-25.5).

## Sum of Squares Formula

From Yates Algorithm:

$$  
SS_{\text{effect}} = \frac{(\text{Contrast})^2}{N \times n}  
$$

Alternative:

$$  
SS_{\text{effect}} = \frac{n \times 2^k(k-2) \times (\text{Effect})^2}{k}  
$$

## Decision Rules

- If $F > F_{\text{critical}}$ or $p < 0.05$, effect is significant
- Use normal probability plot for unreplicated designs
- Effects deviating from straight line are significant

Chapter 06

# Main Effects and Interaction Calculations

## Main Effect Formula

$$  
\text{Effect} = \hat{y}*{\text{high}} - \hat{y}*{\text{low}}  
$$

Average response at high level minus average response at low level

**Example: Main Effect of A**

$$  
A = \left[ \left(a + ab + ac + abc\right) - \left( (1) + b + c + bc \right) \right] / 4  
$$

## Two-Factor Interaction Formula

$$  
AB = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left( A_{\text{effect}} \mid B + \right) - \left( A_{\text{effect}} \mid B - \right) \right]  
$$

Half the difference between A's effect at high B and A's effect at low B

**Example: AB Interaction**

$$  
AB = \left[ ab - b - a + (1) \right] / 2  
$$

## Interpretation Guidelines

↑ Positive Effect

Increasing factor level increases response

↓ Negative Effect

Increasing factor level decreases response

= Near Zero

Factor has negligible effect on response

## Interaction Interpretation

✗ Zero Interaction

Effect of one factor is consistent regardless of other factor

≠ Non-Zero Interaction

Effect of one factor depends on level of other factor

⊆ Large Interaction

Factors must be considered together, not independently
