# What Are Fractional Factorial Designs?

- **Subset of Full Factorial Runs**
A fractional factorial design consists of a carefully chosen subset (fraction) of the experimental runs of a full 2k factorial design.

- **Primary Purpose**
Enables the study of many factors with significantly fewer trials, making it ideal for initial screening experiments.

- **The Strategic Trade-off**
Offers a dramatic reduction in runs (cost/time) at the expense of confounding certain effects (aliasing).

- **Typical Use Case**
Most effective in early stages when the number of factors (k) is large and budget is tight (Screening Phase).

# Why Use Fractional Designs?

![img-0.jpeg](img-0.jpeg)

## Combating Exponential Growth

Full factorial runs increase exponentially with factors (2k). For 7 factors, a full design requires 128 runs, while a fractional design can be done in just 8 or 16 runs.

## Resource Efficiency

Drastically reduces time, material costs, and equipment usage. This efficiency makes it feasible to screen many variables even with limited budgets.

## Faster Learning Cycles

Supports sequential experimentation strategies. Instead of one massive experiment, researchers can run smaller fractions to learn quickly and iterate.

## Focus on Vital Effects

Leverages the "Sparsity of Effects" principle: typically only main effects and large two-factor interactions drive process performance, which fractional designs target.

# Confounding and Aliasing — Core Concepts

## Aliasing

Occurs when two or more effects are indistinguishable from each other because they change simultaneously in the design. We estimate the sum of these effects.

## Complete Confounding

When the correlation between two effects is $\pm 1$. In fractional designs, main effects are often deliberately confounded with higher-order interactions.

## Partial Confounding

Occurs in blocked designs where effects are confounded with block differences, or when design orthogonality is lost.

# Constructing a Half Fraction 2k-1

## Choose Generators

Select generators to define the fraction. For a 2k-1 design, choose the highest-order interaction (e.g., D = ABC for k = 4) to minimize aliasing of main effects.

## Create Defining Relation

Establish the defining relation I. This is formed by the product of the generator columns (e.g., I = ABCD). It determines the aliasing structure of the design.

## Build Design Matrix

Construct the basic design matrix for k-1 factors first (as a full factorial). Then, calculate the levels for the k-th factor using the generator relationship (e.g., D = A × B × C).

## Finalize Experimental Plan

Randomize the run order to mitigate nuisance variables. Include replicates or center points if necessary to estimate experimental error and check for curvature.

# Design Resolution (III, IV, V)

Resolution indicates the degree of confounding in a design. It is defined by the length of the shortest word in the defining relation.

|  Resolution | Description & Confounding Pattern | Practical Implication  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  III | **HIGH CONFOUNDING**
Main effects are aliased with 2-Factor Interactions (2FIs).
Example: A = BC | Use only for initial screening when you assume interactions are negligible. Risky if interactions are real.  |
|  IV | **MODERATE CONFOUNDING**
Main effects are clear of 2FIs.
2FIs are aliased with other 2FIs.
Example: A is clear, but AB = CD | Standard for screening. Allows estimation of main effects without bias from 2FIs. Need fold-over to separate 2FIs.  |
|  V | **LOW CONFOUNDING**
Main effects and 2FIs are clear of each other.
2FIs are aliased with 3FIs.
Example: AB = CDE | Excellent for characterization and optimization. Requires more runs but provides detailed insight into interactions.  |

# Defining Relations and Alias Structures

## The Defining Relation

Constructed from the design generators. For a 24-1 design where $D = ABC$, the defining relation is $I = ABCD$. "I" acts as the identity element (like 1 in multiplication).

## Calculating Aliases

Multiply any effect by the defining relation to find its alias.

Example: Effect $A \times I = A \times ABCD = A_2BCD = BCD$.

## Interpretation

The calculated coefficient actually estimates the sum of the effect and its aliases. If we calculate "AB", we are actually estimating \textbf{AB} + \textbf{CD}.

## Alias Derivation Example (Resolution IV: I = ABCD)

1. One-factor Interaction Example:

```
A   ×   ABCD   =   A²BCD → BCD   (Clear of 2FIs)
```

2. Two-Factor Interaction Example:

```
AB   ×   ABCD   =   A²B²CD → CD   (Confounded!)
```

Resulting Alias Chains (What we actually estimate)

- [A] → A + BCD
- [B] → B + ACD
- [AB] → AB + CD

# Example Setup: 27-4 Screening Design

## Primary Objective
Screen 7 distinct factors (A through G) to identify significant effects using only 16 experimental runs.

## Fractional Efficiency
Utilizes a 1/8th fraction of the full factorial design (27 = 128 runs), drastically reducing resource requirements while maintaining statistical validity for screening.

## Generator Selection
Select 4 independent generators to define the fraction, targeting at least Resolution IV design to ensure main effects are clear of 2-factor interactions.

## Execution Plan
Randomize the run order to minimize bias, include replicates for pure error estimation, and add 2–4 center points to check for curvature.

# 2(7-4) Example — Analysis Roadmap

## 1. Effect Estimation

Calculate contrast sums for all 15 effects. Construct a half-normal or normal probability plot to visually separate vital few effects from trivial many (noise).

## 2. Significance Screening

Use Lenth's method (PSE) or pooled error from smallest effects to determine statistical significance (t-tests/ANOVA) without replicates.

## 3. Alias Interpretation

Consult alias structure (e.g., A + BCD). If 'A' is significant, is it really A or the interaction BCD? Apply sparsity of effects principle (main effects &gt; interactions).

## 4. Decision: Confirm or Fold?

If ambiguity remains (e.g., aliased 2FIs), perform a fold-over or run confirmation trials to resolve.

# Application Case I (Manufacturing)

## Objective

Reduce surface roughness (Ra) in a CNC turning operation to improve component finish and reduce downstream polishing.

## Factors &amp; Levels

- Spindle Speed (A)
- Depth of Cut (C)
- Coolant Type (E)
- Material Batch (G)
- Feed Rate (B)
- Tool Nose Radius (D)
- Fixture Type (F)
- Operator (H)

## Design Setup

Design Type: 2^(7-4) Fractional Factorial (Resolution IV)
Runs: 16 Runs + 2 Center Points (Total 18 runs)
Generators: D=AB, E=AC, F=BC, G=ABC

## Analysis &amp; Outcome

- Main Effects: Spindle Speed (A) and Feed Rate (B) were identified as the most significant factors affecting Ra.
- Interactions: A strong interaction was suspected. Since Res IV confounds 2FIs, a fold-over was performed to resolve aliasing.
- Optimization: Settings adjusted to High Speed, Low Feed, and larger Tool Nose Radius.

22% Reduction
In Surface Roughness (Ra)
