LECTURE 31
INTRODUCTION


INTRODUCTION

In Natural Language Processing (NLP), Pragmatics focuses on contextual meaning, speaker intent, and implied meaning beyond literal word interpretation. It enables understanding of sarcasm, indirect requests, politeness, and real-world assumptions.

Discourse Analysis studies how sentences are connected to form coherent texts. It handles reference resolution, topic continuity, and overall text structure across sentences.

Together, pragmatics and discourse allow NLP systems such as chatbots and assistants to understand how language is used in context, not just what words mean individually.


PRAGMATICS IN NLP

Focus:
- Contextual meaning
- Speaker intent
- Implicature
- Real-world knowledge

Goal:
Understand the “why” and “how” of language use in specific situations.

Examples:
- “Can you pass the salt?” interpreted as a polite request.
- Detecting sarcasm or idiomatic expressions.
- Applying Grice’s Maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relevance, Manner).


DISCOURSE ANALYSIS IN NLP

Focus:
- Relationships between sentences
- Coherence across utterances and paragraphs

Goal:
Maintain continuity and connected meaning in text.

Examples:
- Coreference resolution: “She” refers to “Riya”.
- Understanding narrative flow and conversational turn-taking.


HOW PRAGMATICS AND DISCOURSE WORK TOGETHER

Discourse provides structural connections across text, while pragmatics adds intent and situational understanding.

Example:
A chatbot uses discourse analysis to track conversation history and pragmatics to infer user goals (e.g., booking a flight vs. asking for prices).


WHY PRAGMATICS MATTERS

Example 1:
“Can you pull the car over?”
Literal meaning: Are you capable of pulling the car?
Pragmatic meaning: Please stop the car.

Example 2:
“Wow, that’s just what I needed.”
Literal meaning: Positive sentiment.
Pragmatic meaning: Sarcasm depending on context.

Example 3:
“Crack the window.”
Pragmatic meaning: Open the window.


ASPECTS OF PRAGMATICS

1. Deixis  
Words whose meaning depends on context.
Examples:
- me, here, yesterday
- “Meet me here.”

2. Implicature  
More is communicated than explicitly stated.

A. Conversational Implicature  
Depends on cooperative principles.
Example:
A: I am out of gas.  
B: The gas station is around the corner.

B. Conventional Implicature  
Attached directly to words.
Example:
“Abhishek is rich but sad.”

3. Presupposition  
Assumptions made by the speaker.
Example:
“Jane no longer writes fiction.”
(Presupposes Jane once wrote fiction.)

4. Speech Acts  
Sentences that perform actions.
Example:
“I apologize.”

5. Conversational Structure  
Rules governing dialogue flow and turn-taking.


LECTURE 32
SYNTACTIC AND SEMANTIC CONSTRAINTS ON COREFERENCE


COREFERENCE RESOLUTION

Coreference resolution identifies expressions that refer to the same entity.

It is essential for high-performing NLP systems.


DISCOURSE MODEL

Discourse Model:
A mental model built incrementally containing entities, their properties, and relations.

Referent:
The discourse entity itself.

Referring Expression:
The linguistic phrase referring to a referent.

Two expressions referring to the same entity are said to corefer.


ANAPHORA

Anaphora refers to entities already introduced in discourse.

- First mention: Antecedent
- Subsequent mentions: Anaphors
- Single mention entities: Singletons

Example:
“The University of Illinois at Chicago is excellent. UIC has many faculty. The school is located downtown.”

Coreference chain:
{University of Illinois at Chicago, UIC, The school, it}


COREFERENCE CHAINS

A coreference chain is a set of expressions referring to the same entity.

Example:
{“University of Illinois at Chicago”, “UIC”, “The school”, “it”}


SYNTACTIC AND SEMANTIC CONSTRAINTS


1. Person and Case Agreement

First person: I, we  
Second person: you  
Third person: he, she, they  

Incorrect agreement blocks coreference.

Example:
“You and I have an Acura. We love it.”
“You and I have an Acura. They love it.” (invalid)


2. Gender Agreement

Pronouns must match gender.

Example:
“John has an Acura. He is attractive.”
“John has an Acura. It is attractive.” (it = Acura)


3. Syntactic Constraints

Reflexives must refer to local subjects.

Example:
“John bought himself a car.” ✔
“John bought him a car.” ✖

Constraints depend on sentence structure.


4. Selectional Restrictions

Verb semantics restrict possible referents.

Example:
“John bought a new Acura. It drinks gasoline.” (metaphorical)
“John parked his Acura in the garage. It is messy.” (it = garage)


PREFERENCES IN PRONOUN INTERPRETATION

1. Recency  
Recent entities are more salient.

2. Grammatical Role  
Subject > Object > Others

3. Repeated Mention  
Frequently mentioned entities remain salient.

4. Parallelism  
Pronouns prefer parallel grammatical roles.

5. Verb Semantics (Implicit Causality)
Certain verbs bias interpretation.
- criticize → object biased
- telephone → subject biased


LECTURE 33
LAPPIN AND LEASS’ ALGORITHM FOR PRONOUN RESOLUTION


OVERVIEW

A salience-based algorithm using syntactic constraints and preferences.


KEY COMPONENTS

- Discourse model update
- Coreference equivalence classes
- Salience computation
- Pronoun resolution


SALIENCE WEIGHTS (EXAMPLES)

Recency: 100  
Subject: 80  
Object: 50  
Indirect object: 40  
Head noun: 80  
Parallelism: +35  
Cataphora: -175  

Salience is halved after each sentence.


ALGORITHM STEPS

1. Collect potential referents (up to four sentences back)
2. Remove candidates violating gender/number agreement
3. Apply syntactic constraints
4. Add salience values
5. Select referent with highest salience
6. Break ties using proximity


EXAMPLE

John saw a beautiful Acura Integra.
He showed it to Bob.
He bought it.

Resolved references:
- he → John
- it → Acura Integra


CENTERING THEORY (EXTENSION)

Introduces discourse centers:
- Backward-looking center (Cb)
- Forward-looking centers (Cf)
- Preferred center (Cp)

Transition preferences:
Continue > Retain > Smooth-Shift > Rough-Shift
