LECTURE 31
SYNTACTIC AND SEMANTIC CONSTRAINTS ON COREFERENCE


REFERENCE RESOLUTION

Reference resolution is the task of identifying all noun phrases that refer to the same real-world entity within a discourse.


NUMBER AGREEMENT

Referring expressions and their referents must agree in number.

Examples:
- John has a new Acura. It is red.
- John has three new Acuras. They are red.

Incorrect:
- John has a new Acura. They are red.
- John has three new Acuras. It is red.


PERSON AND CASE AGREEMENT

Person agreement requires consistency in grammatical person.

First person: I, we  
Second person: you  
Third person: he, she, they  

Examples:
- You and I have an Acura. We love it.
- John and Mary have Acuras. They love them.

Incorrect:
- John and Mary have Acuras. We love them.
- You and I have an Acura. They love it.


GENDER AGREEMENT

Pronouns must agree in gender with their referents.

Examples:
- John has an Acura. He is attractive. (he = John)
- John bought an Acura. It is attractive. (it = Acura)


SYNTACTIC CONSTRAINTS

Coreference is constrained by syntactic structure, especially within the same sentence.

Reflexives:
- himself, herself, themselves

Examples:
- John bought himself a new Acura. ✔
- John bought him a new Acura. ✖

- John said Bill bought him a new Acura. (him ≠ Bill)
- John said Bill bought himself a new Acura. (himself = Bill)

Additional examples:
- John wanted a new car. Bill bought him a new Acura. (him = John)
- John wanted a new car. He bought him a new Acura. (he = John, him ≠ John)
- John placed the pamphlets next to himself. (himself = John)
- John placed the pamphlets next to him. (him = John)


SELECTIONAL RESTRICTIONS

Verbs impose semantic constraints on their arguments, which can eliminate incorrect referents.

Example:
- John bought a new Acura. It drinks gasoline. (metaphorical violation)

World knowledge is often required:
- John parked his Acura in the garage. It is messy. (it = garage)
- John parked his Acura in Beverly Hills. It is messy. (it = Beverly Hills)


LECTURE 32
PREFERENCES IN PRONOUN INTERPRETATION


RECENCY

Recently mentioned entities are more salient.

Example:
- John has an Integra. Bill has a Legend. Mary likes to drive it.
(it = Legend)


GRAMMATICAL ROLE

Salience hierarchy:
Subject > Object > Others

Examples:
- John went to the dealership with Bill. He bought an Integra. (he = John)
- Bill went to the dealership with John. He bought an Integra. (he = Bill)
- Bill and John went to the dealership. He bought an Integra. (ambiguous)


REPEATED MENTION

Entities mentioned repeatedly remain salient.

Example:
- John needed a car. He wanted something sporty.
- Bill went with him to the dealership.
- He bought an Integra.
(he = John)


PARALLELISM

Parallel grammatical structures influence pronoun interpretation.

Example:
- Mary went with Sue to the Acura dealership.
- Sally went with her to the Mazda dealership.
(her = Sue)

However, parallelism is not absolute:
- Mary went with Sue to the dealership.
- Sally warned her not to buy anything.
(her = Mary)


VERB SEMANTICS (IMPLICIT CAUSALITY)

Certain verbs bias pronoun interpretation.

Examples:
- John telephoned Bill. He lost the pamphlet. (he = John)
- John criticized Bill. He lost the pamphlet. (he = Bill)

Thematic role bias:
- John seized the pamphlet from Bill. He likes reading about cars. (he = John)
- John passed the pamphlet to Bill. He likes reading about cars. (he = Bill)

- The dealer admired John. He knows cars well. (he = John)
- The dealer impressed John. He knows cars well. (he = dealer)


LECTURE 33
LAPPIN AND LEASS’ ALGORITHM FOR PRONOUN RESOLUTION


OVERVIEW

A salience-based algorithm for pronoun resolution using syntactic constraints and preferences.


KEY COMPONENTS

- Discourse model update
- Evoked entities
- Coreference equivalence classes
- Salience computation
- Pronoun resolution


SALIENCE WEIGHTS (EXAMPLE VALUES)

Recency: 100  
Subject: 80  
Existential: 70  
Object: 50  
Indirect object / oblique: 40  
Non-adverbial PP: 50  
Head noun: 80  
Parallelism: +35  
Cataphora: −175  

Salience values are halved after each sentence.


ARGUMENT ORDERING

Implicit salience order:
subject > existential predicate > object > indirect object > adverbial PP

Example:
On the sofa, the cat was eating bonbons.

- sofa: 100 + 40 + 80 = 220
- cat: 100 + 80 + 50 + 80 = 310
- bonbons: 100 + 50 + 50 + 80 = 280


SALIENCE UPDATE EXAMPLE

Sentence:
The bonbons were very tasty.

Updated salience:
- sofa: 70
- cat: 155
- bonbons: 450

Sentence:
They were a gift from an admirer.

Updated salience:
- sofa: 35
- cat: 77.5
- bonbons: 225 (+35)


REFERENCE RESOLUTION PROCEDURE

1. Collect potential antecedents (up to four sentences back)
2. Remove candidates failing gender/number agreement
3. Remove candidates violating syntactic constraints
4. Add salience bonuses or penalties
5. Select the referent with highest salience
6. Break ties using proximity


SOLUTION EXAMPLES

They were a gift from an admirer.
(they = bonbons)

Reason:
- Only plural candidate
- Highest salience

It ate them all.
(it = cat)


CENTERING THEORY (EXTENSION)

Introduces discourse centers:
- Backward-looking center Cb
- Forward-looking centers Cf
- Preferred center Cp

Transition preferences:
Continue > Retain > Smooth-Shift > Rough-Shift


ALGORITHM SUMMARY

- Generate all possible Cb–Cf combinations
- Apply syntactic and semantic constraints
- Rank transitions by preference
- Select best reference assignment


FINAL EXAMPLE

John saw a beautiful Acura Integra.
He showed it to Bob.
He bought it.

Resolved:
- he → John
- it → Acura Integra
