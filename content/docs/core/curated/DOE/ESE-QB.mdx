<LongQuestion>
### Q1. Explain why the One-Variable-at-a-Time (OVAT) approach is inefficient in experimentation. <QuestionDot color="orange" text="Important" />
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

The <Highlight color="pink">**One-Variable-at-a-Time (OVAT)**</Highlight> approach means changing **one factor at a time** while keeping all others fixed, and repeating this separately for every factor.

**Why it is inefficient:**

**Problem 1 - Misses Interactions:** In real processes, two factors often influence each other. OVAT can never detect this because it never tests factors **together** simultaneously. Missing an interaction means the findings are incomplete and potentially wrong.

**Problem 2 - More Runs Wasted:** OVAT requires far more experiments than necessary. For example, studying 3 factors at 2 levels with OVAT requires many separate test series, whereas a 2³ full factorial design covers **all combinations in just 8 structured runs**.

**Problem 3 - Cannot Find the True Optimum:** Since interactions are ignored, the best combination of factor settings is never discovered. The result found by OVAT may look reasonable but is rarely the **global optimum**.

**Quick OVAT vs DoE Summary (Exam Points):**

- **Factor handling:** OVAT changes one factor at a time; DoE changes factors together in a planned pattern.
- **Interaction detection:** OVAT cannot detect interactions; DoE identifies and quantifies interactions.
- **Experimental effort:** OVAT usually needs many runs; DoE gives maximum information with fewer structured runs.
- **Optimization quality:** OVAT often gives a local best setting; DoE is more likely to find the true optimum.
- **Result reliability:** OVAT conclusions are weaker; DoE conclusions are statistically stronger and more reproducible.

In contrast, DoE studies **all factors simultaneously** in a structured way, finds interactions, reaches the true optimum, and does it in far fewer runs. This is why OVAT is considered inefficient and DoE is preferred in industrial experimentation.

<LongQuestion>
### Q2. Define randomization, replication, and blocking with suitable examples. <QuestionDot color="orange" text="Important" />
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

The basic principles of Design of Experiments (DoE) ensure that experiments are **efficient**, **reliable**, and **scientifically valid**.

The first principle is <Highlight color="green">**Randomization**</Highlight>. This means doing experiments in a **random order** so that outside factors do not affect the results. For example, running tests randomly reduces problems caused by machine wear or changes in the environment over time. Without randomization, a factor that happens to be tested last may appear worse simply because the machine had warmed up.

The second principle is <Highlight color="green">**Replication**</Highlight>. This means **repeating the same experiment** several times. Repeating trials helps us see how much the results vary and makes findings more accurate. For example, testing the strength of a product many times under the same conditions shows how consistent it is and allows proper error estimation.

The third principle is <Highlight color="green">**Blocking**</Highlight>. This involves **grouping similar experimental units** together to remove the effect of known factors that are not being studied. For example, grouping experiments by machine or work shift helps focus on the main factors being tested and prevents nuisance variation from distorting results.

These three principles together improve the **reliability and validity** of experimental results in industrial applications.

<LongQuestion>
### Q3. What are degrees of freedom? Illustrate with an example.
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

<Highlight color="pink">**Degrees of Freedom (DOF)**</Highlight> are the **number of independent pieces of information** available to estimate effects or error. In DoE, they indicate how many factor effects or interactions can be estimated independently.

**General Rule:** DOF for a factor = (Number of levels of that factor) - 1.

**For a two-factor, two-level (2²) design:**
- Factors: A and B, each at 2 levels
- Total runs: $2 \times 2 = 4$
- Total DOF: $N - 1 = 4 - 1 = 3$
- DOF for A: $2 - 1 = 1$
- DOF for B: $2 - 1 = 1$
- DOF for interaction AB: $(2-1)(2-1) = 1$

So, $3 = 1 + 1 + 1$.

**For a 2³ design (3 factors, 8 runs):**
- Total DOF = $8 - 1 = 7$
- DOF for A, B, C = 1 each (total 3)
- DOF for AB, AC, BC = 1 each (total 3)
- DOF for ABC = 1
- Total = $3 + 3 + 1 = 7$ ✓

With no replication, all degrees of freedom are used up by effects and interactions, leaving **no pure error DOF**. Adding replication creates extra DOF which allows proper error estimation and significance testing.

In general, more DOF means more information available, leading to more reliable experimental conclusions.

<LongQuestion>
### Q4. What is confounding in DOE?
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

<Highlight color="pink">**Confounding**</Highlight> (also called **aliasing**) occurs when the effect of one factor is **mixed together** with the effect of another factor or interaction. Because their results are mathematically combined, it becomes impossible to tell which factor actually caused the change in the response.

Confounding is a common trade-off. Engineers **accept it intentionally** when they use fractional designs (fewer test runs) to save time and money.

**Confounding Severity — Design Resolution:**

- **Resolution III:** Main effects are mixed with 2-factor interactions. Use with caution - only good for initial screening.
- **Resolution IV:** Main effects are clear, but 2-factor interactions are mixed with each other.

**Example:**
In a fractional design, the estimated effect of Factor A may actually represent:

`Estimated Effect = (True Effect of A) + (Interaction of B × C)`

If the B×C interaction is strong, it inflates the estimate for A. An engineer who ignores confounding may mistakenly change Factor A in production and achieve nothing useful. This is why understanding confounding is essential before making any process decisions.

<LongQuestion>
### Q5. Explain the role of statistical thinking in industrial experimentation.
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

<Highlight color="green">**Statistical thinking**</Highlight> is the foundation of good industrial experimentation. It focuses on understanding **variation**, collecting evidence, and making decisions based on **data rather than assumptions**.

In industrial processes, outputs always vary. Some variation comes from the factors being studied (signal), and some comes from random noise. Statistical thinking helps engineers **separate real effects from random noise**, so that only true cause-effect relationships are acted upon.

**Key roles of statistical thinking in DoE:**

- **Planning:** Helps choose the right design, number of runs, and factor levels to get reliable answers efficiently without wasting resources.
- **Analysis:** Uses tools like ANOVA, regression, and probability plots to objectively identify significant factors from data.
- **Decision-making:** Supports conclusions with calculated confidence levels (e.g., 95% confidence) rather than gut feeling.
- **Error estimation:** Quantifies how much variability exists in the process so that results are not over-interpreted.
- **Model Building:** Statistical thinking enables building a **prediction equation** (regression model) that links factor settings to the expected response value.

**Statistical, Technical, and Sociological Dimensions:**
DoE success depends on all three:
- **Statistical dimension:** Correct design selection and valid analysis methods.
- **Technical dimension:** Deep process knowledge to choose meaningful factors and levels.
- **Sociological dimension:** Team cooperation, communication, and management support to implement findings.

**Example:** In a plastic moulding process, statistical analysis of a factorial experiment shows that temperature has a p-value of 0.02 (significant) while cooling speed has a p-value of 0.45 (not significant). Without statistics, both factors might have been changed unnecessarily.

Therefore, statistical thinking ensures that industrial experiments are **objective, reproducible, and cost-effective**.

<LongQuestion>
### Q6. Differentiate between main effects and interaction effects. <QuestionDot color="orange" text="Important" />
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

**Main Effect:**

A <Highlight color="green">**main effect**</Highlight> is the **individual impact of a single factor** on the response, averaged across all levels of other factors.

It is calculated as:

$$
\text{Main Effect of A} = \overline{Y}_{A+} - \overline{Y}_{A-}
$$

(Average response when A is High) minus (Average response when A is Low).

**Example:** If tensile strength is 70 at high temperature and 50 at low temperature, main effect of temperature = 70 − 50 = **+20**.

---

**Interaction Effect:**

An <Highlight color="pink">**interaction effect**</Highlight> occurs when the **effect of one factor depends on the level of another factor**. Factors do not act independently — their combined influence on the response is different from the sum of their individual effects.

**Example:** Temperature may improve strength by +20 at low pressure, but by only +5 at high pressure. This difference in behaviour is an interaction.

| Feature | Main Effect | Interaction Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Involves** | One factor | Two or more factors |
| **Graph** | Simple bar or line | Lines that cross or diverge |
| **Can be misleading if** | Interactions are present | Ignored in analysis |
| **Estimated using** | Average difference | Product of sign columns |

Ignoring interactions while focusing only on main effects can lead to **wrong process optimization decisions**. Therefore, both must always be studied together in DoE.

<LongQuestion>
### Q7. Explain synergistic and antagonistic interactions with examples.
</LongQuestion>

**(5 or 10 Marks)**

Interaction effects in DoE can be classified as **synergistic** or **antagonistic**, depending on how factors influence the response when combined.

**Synergistic Interaction**

A synergistic interaction occurs when the **combined effect of two factors is greater than the sum of their individual effects**. The factors reinforce each other and produce an enhanced response. For example, increasing both temperature and pressure together may significantly improve product strength more than expected from their individual effects alone.

Synergistic interactions are desirable in many processes because they lead to improved performance and efficiency.

---

**Antagonistic Interaction**

An antagonistic interaction occurs when the **combined effect of two factors is less than the sum of their individual effects**. One factor reduces or counteracts the effect of the other. For example, increasing temperature may improve yield, but when combined with high speed, the overall yield may actually decrease.

Antagonistic interactions indicate conflict between factors and help identify combinations that should be avoided.

---

| Feature | Synergistic (Helpful) | Antagonistic (Harmful) |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Result is **greater** than individual effects added up | Result is **less** than individual effects |
| **Graph Lines** | Lines flare outward; factors amplify each other | Lines cross over; factors cancel each other |
| **How to Use** | Maximize both inputs for best output | Find a safe "sweet spot" to avoid conflict |
| **Example** | Heat + Good Catalyst = Enormous yield | High speed + insufficient lubrication = overheating/burning of surface |

**Important Rule:** On interaction graphs, <Highlight color="blue">**parallel lines mean no interaction**</Highlight>. If lines cross or angle away from each other, an interaction is present.

**Conclusion**

Understanding whether interactions are synergistic or antagonistic helps engineers select proper factor combinations, optimize processes, and avoid undesirable operating conditions. Hence, interaction analysis plays a crucial role in effective experimental design.

### Q8. Discuss barriers in successful application of DOE in industries. <QuestionDot color="red" text="10 Marks" />
</LongQuestion>

**(10 Marks)**

DoE often fails in practice due to combined **statistical, technical, and sociological barriers**.

**1. Statistical Barrier**

Weak understanding of concepts like interaction, variance, and model assumptions. This causes wrong design choice or incorrect interpretation of results. Engineers may treat a confounded effect as a true main effect and make wrong process decisions.

**2. Technical Barrier**

Limited process control, poor measurement systems, or expensive experiments. If factors cannot be set precisely and repeatedly, DoE conclusions become unreliable. A poorly calibrated instrument alone can ruin an otherwise well-planned experiment.

**3. Sociological Barrier**

Resistance to change, low management support, and poor cross-team communication. Teams may prefer familiar trial-and-error over structured experiments. Without management buy-in, resources and time are not allocated for proper DoE.

**Step 1 — Define the Objective:** Clearly state what you want to improve or optimize (e.g., reduce surface roughness, increase yield).

**Step 2 — Select Factors and Levels:** Identify which input variables to study and set their Low (−1) and High (+1) levels based on engineering knowledge.

**Step 3 — Choose the Right Design:** Decide between full factorial, fractional factorial, or screening design based on the number of factors and available resources.

**Step 4 — Run the Experiment:** Conduct runs in **random order** to avoid bias. Record the response variable accurately for each run.

**Step 5 — Analyze Results:** Use statistical tools to identify significant factors and interactions.

**Step 6 — Confirm and Implement:** Run a confirmation experiment at the optimum settings. If results match predictions, implement the settings in production.

---

#### Part B — Analytical Tools Used in DoE

**1. Pareto Plot:**
A bar chart showing factor effects ranked from largest to smallest. A reference line marks the boundary of statistical significance. Factors crossing the line are the **vital few** that truly matter.

**2. Interaction Plot:**
A line graph showing how the effect of one factor changes at different levels of another factor. **Parallel lines = no interaction. Crossing or diverging lines = interaction present.**

**3. Normal Probability Plot:**
Plots calculated effects on a normal probability scale. Effects that fall off the straight line are statistically significant and are not due to random noise.

**4. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance):**
Partitions total variation in the response into contributions from each factor and error. Produces **p-values** to formally test which factors are significant.

**5. Main Effects Plot:**
Shows the average response at each level of every factor. A steep slope means the factor has a large main effect.

These tools together give a complete picture of which factors to control and at what levels to optimize the process.

<LongQuestion>
### Q11 / Q12. Construct a 2² full factorial design and estimate main and interaction effects. / Construct a 2³ full factorial design and explain estimation of main effects. <QuestionDot color="orange" text="Important" />
</LongQuestion>

**(5 or 10 Marks)**

#### Part A — 2² Full Factorial Design

A **2² full factorial design** tests **2 factors** at **2 levels**, requiring **N = 2² = 4 runs**. It finds main effects and the interaction between the two factors.

**Example:** Optimizing surface finish on a machining process.
- **Factor A:** Cutting Speed (Low '−' / High '+')
- **Factor B:** Feed Rate (Low '−' / High '+')
- **Response:** Surface Roughness (Ra)

**Design Matrix:**

| Run | A | B | AB | Response (Ra) |
|-----|---|---|----|---------------|
| 1   | − | − | +  | y₁            |
| 2   | + | − | −  | y₂            |
| 3   | − | + | −  | y₃            |
| 4   | + | + | +  | y₄            |

*(+ = High, − = Low)*

**Estimating Effects:**

$$
\text{Effect of A} = \frac{(y_2 + y_4) - (y_1 + y_3)}{2}
$$

$$
\text{Effect of B} = \frac{(y_3 + y_4) - (y_1 + y_2)}{2}
$$

$$
\text{Interaction AB} = \frac{(y_1 + y_4) - (y_2 + y_3)}{2}
$$

A negative interaction value means raising both factors together gives a **worse-than-expected** benefit — this is an <Highlight color="blue">**antagonistic interaction**</Highlight>.

#### Part B — 2³ Full Factorial Design

A **2³ full factorial design** tests **3 factors** at **2 levels**, requiring **N = 2³ = 8 runs**. It reveals all main effects and all two-factor and three-factor interactions.

**Example:** Improving chemical yield with factors A (Temperature), B (Pressure), C (Catalyst).

**Design Matrix:**

<Callout type="info" title="Standard Alternating Pattern">
- **Column A:** Changes every 1 row (`− + − + − + − +`)
- **Column B:** Changes every 2 rows (`− − + + − − + +`)
- **Column C:** Changes every 4 rows (`− − − − + + + +`)
</Callout>

| Run | A  | B  | C  | Response |
|-----|----|----|----|----------|
| 1   | −  | −  | −  | Y₁       |
| 2   | +  | −  | −  | Y₂       |
| 3   | −  | +  | −  | Y₃       |
| 4   | +  | +  | −  | Y₄       |
| 5   | −  | −  | +  | Y₅       |
| 6   | +  | −  | +  | Y₆       |
| 7   | −  | +  | +  | Y₇       |
| 8   | +  | +  | +  | Y₈       |

*(+ = High, − = Low)*

**Estimating Main Effects:**

$$
\text{Effect of A} = \frac{(Y_2+Y_4+Y_6+Y_8)-(Y_1+Y_3+Y_5+Y_7)}{4}
$$

$$
\text{Effect of B} = \frac{(Y_3+Y_4+Y_7+Y_8)-(Y_1+Y_2+Y_5+Y_6)}{4}
$$

$$
\text{Effect of C} = \frac{(Y_5+Y_6+Y_7+Y_8)-(Y_1+Y_2+Y_3+Y_4)}{4}
$$

Each main effect = (average response at '+' level) − (average response at '−' level) for that factor. The factor with the **largest absolute effect value** is the most influential.

### Q13 / Q14. Explain the steps in designing a Plackett-Burman screening experiment. / Using a Plackett-Burman design, show how significant factors are identified. <QuestionDot color="red" text="10 Marks" />
</LongQuestion>

**(10 Marks)**

**Plackett-Burman (P-B) designs** are two-level screening tests used to identify the most important factors quickly. The total number of runs **N is always a multiple of 4** (like 8, 12, 16). They allow studying up to **k = N − 1 factors** in exactly N runs using <Highlight color="green">**orthogonal arrays**</Highlight>.

---

#### Steps in Designing a P-B Experiment

**Step 1 — Define the Objective:** State the response to be measured (e.g., product hardness, yield).

**Step 2 — List All Potential Factors:** Brainstorm all variables that might affect the response. Suppose there are 7 factors (A to G).

**Step 3 — Choose Run Size N:** Select the smallest N that is a multiple of 4 and satisfies N ≥ k + 1. For 7 factors, choose **N = 8**.

**Step 4 — Construct the Design Matrix using the Cyclic Shift Method:**

<Callout type="info" title="The '3-1-1-2' Rule for N = 8">
- **Row 1:** Use the seed row: `+ + + − + − −`
- **Rows 2 to 7:** Shift the previous row one step to the right (last element moves to front).
- **Row 8:** All Low (`− − − − − − −`).
</Callout>

#### Complete 8-Run Matrix (7 Factors: A to G)

| Run | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | Response |
|-----|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|----------|
| 1   | + | + | + | − | + | − | − | y₁ |
| 2   | − | + | + | + | − | + | − | y₂ |
| 3   | − | − | + | + | + | − | + | y₃ |
| 4   | + | − | − | + | + | + | − | y₄ |
| 5   | − | + | − | − | + | + | + | y₅ |
| 6   | + | − | + | − | − | + | + | y₆ |
| 7   | + | + | − | + | − | − | + | y₇ |
| 8   | − | − | − | − | − | − | − | y₈ |

*(+ = High, − = Low)*

**Step 5 — Run the Experiment Randomly:** Conduct all 8 runs in random order and record the response.

---

#### How Significant Factors Are Identified

**Step 6 — Calculate Main Effects:** For each factor, compute:

$$
\text{Effect} = \overline{Y}_{+} - \overline{Y}_{-}
$$

**Step 7 — Use a Pareto Plot or Normal Probability Plot:** Factors whose bars cross the significance reference line on the Pareto plot — or fall off the straight line on a normal plot — are identified as **significant**.

**Step 8 — Focus on Vital Few Factors:** Only the significant factors (often 2 to 3 out of 7) are carried forward for detailed optimization.

<LongQuestion>
### Q15. Explain how variability in process yield can be analysed using a 2³ factorial design.
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

In a **2³ full factorial design**, three factors are each tested at two levels, giving **8 experimental runs**. By replicating the runs, it becomes possible to estimate both the **average response** and the **variability** at each set of conditions.

**Steps to Analyse Variability in Yield:**

**Step 1 — Replicate the Design:** Run the 8-run design at least twice (n = 2 replicates). This gives two yield observations at every factor combination.

**Step 2 — Compute Mean and Variance per Run:** For each run, calculate the average yield $\bar{y}$ and the variance $s²$ across replicates.

**Step 3 — Apply ANOVA:** Use Analysis of Variance to partition total variation in yield into:
- Variation due to Factor A (e.g., Temperature)
- Variation due to Factor B (e.g., Pressure)
- Variation due to Factor C (e.g., Catalyst)
- Variation due to interactions (AB, AC, BC, ABC)
- Residual (random) error

**Step 4 — Identify Variance Drivers:** Factors with large F-values and small p-values (p < 0.05) are statistically significant contributors to variability.

**Step 5 — Optimise for Low Variability:** Set significant factors at levels that both **maximise average yield** and **minimise spread**. This leads to a robust process that performs consistently even under varying conditions.

<LongQuestion>
### Q16. Explain design resolution in fractional factorial designs.
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

<Highlight color="pink">**Design Resolution**</Highlight> describes how much confounding (aliasing) exists between effects in a fractional factorial design. A **higher resolution** means that main effects and interactions are more clearly separated.

Resolution is expressed in Roman numerals:

| Resolution | What is Confounded | Practical Use |
|---|---|---|
| **III** | Main effects mixed with 2-factor interactions | Quick screening only — use with caution |
| **IV** | Main effects are clear; 2-factor interactions mixed with each other | Moderate screening with safe main effects |
| **V** | Main effects and 2-factor interactions are all clear | Suitable for optimization |

  <div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-2">
    <ShieldAlert className="size-6" />
    <span className="text-sm font-medium">Resolution III</span>
  </div>
  <div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-2">
    <ShieldAlert className="size-6" />
    <span className="text-sm font-medium">Resolution IV</span>
  </div>
  <div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-2">
    <CheckCircle2 className="size-6" />
    <span className="text-sm font-medium">Resolution V</span>
  </div>

**Key Rule:** The resolution equals the **length of the shortest word in the defining relation**. For example, if the defining relation is $I = ABCDE$, the design is Resolution V because ABCDE has 5 letters.

**Example:** A 2⁷⁻⁴ design is Resolution III. Some main effects are aliased with 2-factor interactions. It is only suitable for initial screening where interactions are assumed small.

Engineers choose resolution based on the trade-off between **run cost** and the **level of detail** needed from the experiment.

### Q17 / Q18. Illustrate the construction of a half-fractional factorial design. / Construct a 2⁽⁵⁻¹⁾ fractional factorial design and show alias structure. <QuestionDot color="red" text="10 Marks" />
</LongQuestion>

**(10 Marks)**

A **fractional factorial design** uses a selected subset of full factorial runs to reduce effort while preserving key information about main effects and important interactions.

---

#### Constructing a 2⁽⁵⁻¹⁾ Half-Fraction Design

For 5 factors at 2 levels:
- Full factorial: $2^5 = 32$ runs
- Half fraction: $2^{5-1} = 16$ runs (saves 50% of runs)

**Step 1 — Build the Base Design:** Start with a standard $2^4$ full factorial design for the first 4 factors: **A, B, C, D** (16 runs).

| Run | A | B | C | D |
|-----|---|---|---|---|
| 1   | − | − | − | − |
| 2   | + | − | − | − |
| 3   | − | + | − | − |
| 4   | + | + | − | − |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| 16  | + | + | + | + |

**Step 2 — Define the 5th Factor:** Assign Factor **E** using the generator:

$$E = ABCD$$

Each run's sign for E is determined by multiplying the signs of A, B, C, and D for that run.

**Step 3 — Write the Defining Relation:**

$$I = ABCDE$$

All aliasing information is derived from this defining relation.

---

#### Alias Structure

To find the alias of any effect, multiply it by the defining relation $I = ABCDE$:

$$A \times ABCDE = A^2BCDE = BCDE \quad \Rightarrow \quad A \text{ is aliased with } BCDE$$
$$AB \times ABCDE = A^2B^2CDE = CDE \quad \Rightarrow \quad AB \text{ is aliased with } CDE$$

**Summary of Key Aliases:**

| Effect | Aliased With |
|--------|-------------|
| A      | BCDE (4-factor interaction) |
| B      | ACDE |
| C      | ABDE |
| D      | ABCE |
| E      | ABCD |
| AB     | CDE (3-factor interaction) |
| AC     | BDE |

Since all main effects are only aliased with **4-factor interactions** (assumed negligible in practice), this is a **Resolution V** design. Main effects and all 2-factor interactions are estimable clearly.

---

#### Trade-off Summary

| | Full 2⁵ | Half 2⁽⁵⁻¹⁾ |
|---|---|---|
| **Runs needed** | 32 | 16 |
| **Cost** | High | Half |
| **Information** | Complete | Main effects + 2FI all clear |
| **Limitation** | None | Some higher-order aliasing |

Fractional factorial designs are **ideal for early-stage experimentation** when resources are limited and higher-order interactions are assumed to be small.

<LongQuestion>
### Q19. Explain the concept of aliasing/confounding in fractional factorial designs.
</LongQuestion>

**(5 Marks)**

In a **fractional factorial design**, not all factor combinations are tested. To compensate for the missing runs, some effects are mathematically **merged together** — this is called <Highlight color="pink">**aliasing**</Highlight> or **confounding**.

**How Aliasing Works:**

When a design has the defining relation $I = ABCDE$, multiplying any effect by this relation gives its **alias** — the effect it is mixed with:

$$A = A \times I = A \times ABCDE = BCDE$$

So the estimated value for A actually contains both the true effect of A **and** the effect of the BCDE four-factor interaction.

**Why It Is Acceptable:**

In most industrial processes, **higher-order interactions (3-factor, 4-factor) are negligibly small**. So aliasing a main effect with a 4-factor interaction (Resolution V) is safe in practice.

**When It Becomes a Problem:**

In a **Resolution III design**, main effects are aliased with 2-factor interactions. If those 2-factor interactions are real and significant, the main effect estimate will be inflated and misleading — leading to wrong process decisions.

**Key Principle:**

Aliasing is a **deliberate trade-off** — fewer runs in exchange for some confounding. The engineer must use process knowledge to confirm whether the assumed negligible aliases are truly unimportant before accepting the results.

<LongQuestion>
### Explain geometric and non-geometric Plackett-Burman screening designs. For a case with many factors and limited runs, why is a P-B design useful? <QuestionDot color="orange" text="Important" /> <QuestionDot color="green" text="Asked in IA2" />
</LongQuestion>

**(10 Marks)**

Plackett-Burman (P-B) designs are two-level screening designs used to identify important factors quickly when many factors are present.

**Geometric view:** Regular $2^k$ factorial designs map to cube/hypercube geometry. A few small P-B cases may look similar, but this interpretation is limited.

**Non-geometric view (practical P-B):** Most P-B designs are built from orthogonal/Hadamard-type matrices with run sizes like 8, 12, 16, 20 (multiples of 4), not necessarily powers of 2. They estimate main effects efficiently while assuming interactions are negligible during screening.

**Why P-B is useful with many factors and limited runs:**
1. **High efficiency:** Up to $N-1$ factors in $N$ runs.
2. **Lower cost/time:** Major reduction in materials and machine time.
3. **Fast prioritization:** Quickly separates critical factors from unimportant ones.
4. **Better follow-up:** After screening, detailed optimization can focus only on significant factors.

**Example:** 11 factors need 2048 runs in full $2^{11}$ design, but a 12-run P-B can screen them initially.
