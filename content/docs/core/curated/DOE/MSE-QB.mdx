### Q1. Explain screening design with suitable example.
**(5 Marks)**

A **screening design** is an experimental strategy that efficiently identifies <Highlight color="green">significant factors</Highlight> from a large pool of potential variables by testing a carefully selected subset of all possible combinations. Its core principle is to separate the **"vital few"** factors that significantly impact the response from the **"trivial many"** that have negligible effects.

When a process has many potential input factors (typically 5 or more), testing all combinations (full factorial) becomes impractical due to exponential run requirements. Screening designs drastically reduce the number of experimental runs while maintaining statistical validity. For example, screening **7 factors** requires only **8 runs** instead of the **128 runs** needed for a full 2⁷ factorial — a <Highlight color="pink">94% reduction in experimental effort</Highlight>.

**Example:** Consider a pharmaceutical tablet manufacturing process with 7 potential variables: binder concentration (A), compression force (B), mixing time (C), lubricant amount (D), granulation speed (E), drying temperature (F), and humidity (G). A screening design with 8 runs is conducted. After analysis, suppose factors A, B, and D show significant effects on tablet hardness. The engineer then focuses follow-up optimization experiments only on these three factors instead of all seven.

Screening designs are the first phase in a sequential strategy — they narrow the focus so that detailed optimization (Response Surface Methodology) can be applied efficiently.

### Q2. What is P-B screening design? Explain with suitable example for N = 8.
**(10 Marks)**

**Plackett-Burman (P-B) designs** are two-level fractional factorial screening designs developed by Robin L. Plackett and J. Peter Burman in 1946. The defining feature is that the number of runs **N is always a multiple of 4** (N = 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 ...), and up to **k = N - 1 factors** can be studied in N experimental runs.

🟢 **(For 5 Marks — stop here after the following paragraph)**

P-B designs are <Highlight color="green">**orthogonal arrays**</Highlight>, meaning each factor is tested equally at high (+1) and low (-1) levels, and the dot product of any two columns equals zero. This ensures main effects are estimated **independently** of each other with minimum variance. P-B designs are **Resolution III**, so main effects are not confounded with each other, but they are aliased with two-factor interactions. Therefore, P-B designs assume that interactions are negligible — they estimate **main effects only**.

#### Construction of an 8-Run P-B Design

The **cyclical construction** method is used:
- **Step 1:** Define the first row using a specific generator pattern: + + + − + − −
- **Step 2:** Each subsequent row is created by shifting the previous row one position to the right (cyclic shift), with the last element wrapping to the first position.
- **Step 3:** The final (8th) row consists entirely of **-1** values.

#### Complete 8-Run P-B Design Matrix for 7 Factors (A to G)

| Run | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | Response |
|-----|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|----------|
| 1   | + | + | + | - | + | - | - | y1 |
| 2   | - | + | + | + | - | + | - | y2 |
| 3   | - | - | + | + | + | - | + | y3 |
| 4   | + | - | - | + | + | + | - | y4 |
| 5   | - | + | - | - | + | + | + | y5 |
| 6   | + | - | + | - | - | + | + | y6 |
| 7   | + | + | - | + | - | - | + | y7 |
| 8   | - | - | - | - | - | - | - | y8 |

*(+ = High level, - = Low level)*

#### Industrial Example — Injection Moulding Process

A plastics engineer wants to screen 7 process variables affecting the **dimensional accuracy** of injection-moulded parts:
- A: Melt Temperature | B: Injection Speed | C: Holding Pressure | D: Cooling Time | E: Mould Temperature | F: Back Pressure | G: Screw Speed

Using the 8-run P-B design above, the engineer runs all 8 trials (in **randomized order**), records the dimensional deviation (response), and computes the **main effect** of each factor.

### Q3. With suitable example explain full factorial designs in DoE.
**(5 Marks)**

A <Highlight color="pink">**full factorial design**</Highlight> is an experimental design that includes **all possible combinations** of factor levels. For k factors each at 2 levels, the total number of experimental runs is **N = 2ᵏ**. Unlike screening designs, full factorial designs estimate **all main effects, all two-factor interactions (2FI), and all higher-order interactions** without any confounding.

Full factorial designs are used when the number of factors is small (≤ 4), interactions are suspected to be important, and resources are adequate. Each additional factor **doubles** the number of runs.

**Example:** Consider a **chemical reaction process** where two factors are studied:
- **Factor A:** Temperature (Low/High)
- **Factor B:** Pressure (Low/High)
- **Response:** Reaction Yield (%)

This is a **2² = 4 run** full factorial design. All four combinations — (A−B−), (A+B−), (A−B+), (A+B+) — are tested. Main effects of A and B, and the AB interaction, can all be estimated independently. This is not possible with OVAT (One-Variable-At-a-Time), which misses the interaction effect entirely.

Full factorial designs provide **complete process knowledge** and form the basis for more advanced designs such as fractional factorials and response surface methods.

### Q4. Give one example of 2² full factorial design.
**(5 Marks)**

A **2² full factorial design** involves **2 factors (k = 2)** at **2 levels each**, resulting in **N = 2² = 4 experimental runs**. All main effects and the two-factor interaction can be estimated.

**Example:** Optimizing surface finish (Ra) in a CNC turning process.
- **Factor A:** Cutting Speed — Low (-) = 100 m/min, High (+) = 200 m/min
- **Factor B:** Feed Rate — Low (-) = 0.1 mm/rev, High (+) = 0.3 mm/rev
- **Response (y):** Surface Roughness Ra (µm)

#### Design Matrix

| Run | A | B | Notation | y (Ra) |
|-----|---|---|----------|--------|
| 1   | - | - | (1)      | 52     |
| 2   | + | - | a        | 67.5   |
| 3   | - | + | b        | 61     |
| 4   | + | + | ab       | 65     |

#### Effect Calculations

$$
\text{Main Effect A} = \frac{(a + ab) - ((1) + b)}{2} = \frac{(67.5 + 65) - (52 + 61)}{2} = \mathbf{9.75}
$$

$$
\text{Main Effect B} = \frac{(b + ab) - ((1) + a)}{2} = \frac{(61 + 65) - (52 + 67.5)}{2} = \mathbf{3.25}
$$

$$
\text{AB Interaction} = \frac{ab - b - a + (1)}{2} = \frac{65 - 61 - 67.5 + 52}{2} = \mathbf{-5.75}
$$

**Interpretation:** Cutting speed (A) has the largest effect on roughness (9.75). The negative AB interaction (-5.75) indicates that increasing both factors simultaneously reduces the expected combined benefit — an <Highlight color="blue">**antagonistic interaction**</Highlight> exists between speed and feed rate.

### Q5. With example explain 2⁴ full factorial design.
**(5 Marks)**

A **2⁴ full factorial design** involves **4 factors (k = 4)** each at **2 levels**, requiring <Highlight color="green">**N = 2⁴ = 16 experimental runs**</Highlight>. It allows estimation of **4 main effects, 6 two-factor interactions, 4 three-factor interactions, and 1 four-factor interaction** — a total of **15 estimable effects**.

**Example:** Optimizing **reaction yield (%)** in a chemical process with 4 factors:
- **A:** Temperature (Low/High)
- **B:** Pressure (Low/High)
- **C:** Catalyst Concentration (Low/High)
- **D:** Reaction Time (Low/High)

#### Design Matrix (Standard Order, 16 Runs)

| Run | A | B | C | D | Notation |
|-----|---|---|---|---|----------|
| 1   | - | - | - | - | (1)      |
| 2   | + | - | - | - | a        |
| 3   | - | + | - | - | b        |
| 4   | + | + | - | - | ab       |
| 5   | - | - | + | - | c        |
| 6   | + | - | + | - | ac       |
| 7   | - | + | + | - | bc       |
| 8   | + | + | + | - | abc      |
| 9   | - | - | - | + | d        |
| 10  | + | - | - | + | ad       |
| 11  | - | + | - | + | bd       |
| 12  | + | + | - | + | abd      |
| 13  | - | - | + | + | cd       |
| 14  | + | - | + | + | acd      |
| 15  | - | + | + | + | bcd      |
| 16  | + | + | + | + | abcd     |

All 16 runs are conducted in **randomized order**. ANOVA is performed to identify which main effects and interactions are statistically significant. Due to the **effect sparsity principle**, typically a few main effects (e.g., A, C) and one or two interactions (e.g., AC) dominate.

The 2⁴ design is the largest practical full factorial — beyond k = 4, **fractional factorial designs** are preferred to reduce run count while sacrificing estimation of higher-order interactions.

### Q6. Explain confounding with example.
**(5 Marks)**

<Highlight color="pink">**Confounding**</Highlight> (also called aliasing) occurs in fractional factorial designs when the effect of one factor or interaction is **mathematically mixed** with another effect, making it impossible to estimate them separately. It is the price paid for reducing experimental runs below the full factorial.

Confounding arises because in a fractional design, we test only a **subset** of all possible combinations. The design generator determines which effects become confounded. The degree of confounding is described by **Design Resolution**:
- **Resolution III:** Main effects confounded with 2-factor interactions.
- **Resolution IV:** Main effects clear; 2-factor interactions confounded with each other.
- **Resolution V:** Main effects and 2-factor interactions are all clear.

This means the **estimated effect of A** actually contains contributions from A itself **plus** the interactions BC, DF, and EG. If any of these interactions are truly significant, the engineer may **wrongly attribute** the effect to main factor A alone.

**Practical implication:** In early-stage screening (Resolution III), confounding is acceptable since interactions are assumed negligible. As the study progresses, higher resolution designs or follow-up experiments are used to **de-alias** significant effects. The golden rule is: *"Never use a low-resolution design when you suspect interactions are present."*

### Q7. Explain importance of selecting quality characteristics for industrial experiments.
**(5 Marks)**

**Quality characteristics** (also called response variables or CTQs — Critical-to-Quality characteristics) are the measurable output variables chosen to evaluate process performance. Selecting the right quality characteristic is a <Highlight color="green">**foundational step**</Highlight> in DoE — if the wrong output is measured, the experiment will fail regardless of how well it is designed.

The selection must follow key guidelines. The characteristic should be **continuous** (measurable on a scale) rather than attribute/binary (pass/fail), because continuous data carries more information, requires smaller sample sizes, and provides higher statistical power to detect factor effects. It should be **directly related to customer requirements** — a characteristic that does not reflect customer needs has no strategic value.

The characteristic must also be **sensitive and monotonic** — it should clearly change in response to factor changes and have a definable direction of improvement. Three standard types are recognized:

| Type | Goal | Example |
|------|------|---------|
| Nominal-the-Best | Hit a target value | Shaft diameter |
| Smaller-the-Better | Minimize | Surface roughness, noise |
| Larger-the-Better | Maximize | Tensile strength, yield |

Finally, the quality characteristic must be **measurable with a valid and calibrated measurement system** (verified through Gage R&R). If measurement error is large relative to process variation, real factor effects will be masked — the experiment will detect noise instead of signal. As stated in the notes: *"If you can't measure it, you can't improve it."*

Proper selection of quality characteristics ensures that experimental resources are invested in answering the right question for the right output.

### Q8. Give the difference between synergistic interaction and antagonistic interaction.
**(5 Marks)**

In DoE, a **two-factor interaction (2FI)** occurs when the effect of one factor on the response depends on the level of another factor. Interactions are classified as either <Highlight color="green">**synergistic**</Highlight> or <Highlight color="pink">**antagonistic**</Highlight> based on their direction and combined impact.

| Parameter | Synergistic Interaction | Antagonistic Interaction |
|-----------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Definition** | Combined effect of two factors **exceeds** the sum of individual effects | Combined effect is **less than** the sum of individual effects |
| **Model Coefficient (bAB)** | **Positive** (+) | **Negative** (-) |
| **Interaction Plot** | Lines diverge (fan-shaped); factors reinforce each other | Lines cross or strongly converge; factors conflict |
| **Nature** | Super-additive; factors complement each other | Sub-additive; factors interfere with each other |
| **Strategy** | Set both factors at High level to maximize response | Find a trade-off "sweet spot"; avoid extreme combined settings |
| **Industrial Example** | High Temperature + Optimized Catalyst ? Yield boosts beyond additive expectation (chemical reactor) | High Cutting Speed + High Feed Rate ? Surface roughness degrades drastically (CNC machining) |
| **IT Example** | Data Caching + Query Optimization ? Throughput multiplies (database) | Heavy Compression + Real-time Encryption ? Latency spikes, throughput drops |

**Key Interpretation Rule:** In interaction plots, <Highlight color="blue">**parallel lines indicate no interaction**</Highlight>; non-parallel or crossing lines signal the presence of synergistic or antagonistic effects. When a significant interaction exists, main effects must **not** be interpreted in isolation — the combination of factor levels must be considered jointly for correct conclusions.

### Q9. Explain any one real-life case study for the application of DoE.
**(10 Marks)**

#### Case Study: Optimizing Surface Hardness in Electronics Component Manufacturing

**Background and Problem Statement:**
In an electronics manufacturing unit, the **surface hardness (HRA)** of metal components was inconsistent, leading to high rejection rates during quality inspection. The traditional One-Variable-At-a-Time (OVAT) approach had failed to identify the root cause. A structured DoE study was initiated to identify and optimize the key process variables.

**Factors and Response Variable:**
Three controllable input factors were identified through team brainstorming using a cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram:
- **A:** Power Potential (P) — energy input to the process
- **B:** Scan Speed (S) — rate of laser/heating movement
- **C:** Quench Rate (Q) — rate of cooling after treatment

The **response variable** selected was Surface Hardness (HRA) — a **continuous, larger-the-better** quality characteristic directly linked to component performance and customer specifications.

**Measurement System Validation:**
Before running experiments, a **Gage R&R (Measurement System Analysis)** was conducted on the hardness tester. The P/T ratio was verified to be below 10%, confirming the measurement system was acceptable.

**Experimental Design:**
A **2³ full factorial design** was selected with N = 8 runs (3 factors × 2 levels each). Each factor was set at a Low (-1) and High (+1) level. Experiments were conducted in **randomized order** to neutralize time-based noise factors such as operator fatigue and ambient temperature variation.

**Data Collection and Analysis:**
After running all 8 trials and recording HRA values, **ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)** was applied to partition total variation into:
- Signal (variation due to factors A, B, C and their interactions)
- Noise (experimental error)

The F-ratio for each effect was computed. Effects with **F > F_critical (p < 0.05)** were declared statistically significant. A **Pareto chart of effects** was used to visually rank the factors.

**Results and Findings:**
- Factor **A (Power Potential)** had the largest main effect — increasing it significantly raised surface hardness.
- Factor **C (Quench Rate)** was the second most significant.
- The **AC interaction** was also significant, indicating a **synergistic interaction**: high power combined with high quench rate produced hardness values far exceeding what each factor achieved alone.
- Factor B (Scan Speed) was found to be non-significant and was removed from the optimized process model.

**Optimal Settings and Confirmation:**
The predicted optimal settings were: **A = High, B = Low (or center), C = High.** A **confirmation run** at these settings was conducted. The observed HRA matched the model prediction within the confidence interval, validating the model.

**Business Outcome:**
After implementing the optimized process settings in the Standard Operating Procedure (SOP):
- Surface hardness rejections reduced by over 60%.
- Process yield and stability improved significantly.
- The study demonstrated that DoE replaced weeks of trial-and-error with a structured 8-run experiment, delivering measurable ROI through **reduced scrap, reduced rework, and improved Cpk**.

This case study illustrates how DoE — through proper factor selection, randomization, ANOVA, and interaction analysis — transforms industrial problem-solving from intuition-based guesswork to **data-driven, statistically validated process improvement**.
