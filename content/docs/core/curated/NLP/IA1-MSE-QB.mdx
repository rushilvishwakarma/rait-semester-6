<Highlight>**Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging**</Highlight> is the process of identifying the correct grammatical category for every word in a sentence. It labels whether a word is a Noun, a Verb, an Adjective, an Adverb, etc.

**Why is it needed?** By knowing the grammatical roles, a computer can understand the literal meaning of a sentence much better.

**Categories of POS Tags:**
- **Open Class words:** These change over time. Every year new words are invented. Examples: Nouns (laptop), Verbs (googling), Adjectives (awesome).
- **Closed Class words:** These are fixed in the English language and rarely change. Examples: Prepositions (in, on), Pronouns (he, she), Conjunctions (and, but).

**The Difficulty of POS Tagging:**
Tagging is difficult because human languages are highly ambiguous. A single word can act as completely different parts of speech depending on where it sits in a sentence.
- Example 1: *"Can you **book** the flight?"* (Here, **book** is a Verb meaning 'to reserve').
- Example 2: *"Please give me the **book**."* (Here, **book** is a Noun meaning 'a physical object to read').

Because POS tagging is difficult, different methods were invented to train computers to do it.

**Three main approaches:**
1. **Rule-Based Tagging:** Uses strict hand-written grammar rules and dictionary lookups.
2. **Statistical Tagging (HMM):** Uses math and probabilities learned from huge amounts of text.
3. **Hybrid Tagging:** Combines both rules and statistics together for best results.

#### Detail on Rule-Based POS Tagging:
This is the oldest method. Human experts manually write hundreds of logical grammar rules for the computer to follow.

It works in two main steps:
- **Step 1 (Dictionary Lookup):** The computer looks at every word in the sentence and gives it EVERY possible tag it has in the dictionary. (e.g. `book` --> Noun OR Verb).
- **Step 2 (Rule Application to Disambiguate):** The computer uses grammar rules to delete the incorrect tags. 

**Example of a Rule:**
*"If the previous word is an article like 'The' or 'A', then the current word is almost certainly a Noun, so delete the Verb option."*
So, in "the book", the rule forcefully throws away the 'Verb' tag, leaving the correct 'Noun' tag.

<Highlight>**Hidden Markov Models (HMM)**</Highlight> represent the Statistical approach to POS Tagging. Instead of a human writing strict rules, the computer calculates the probability of what the tag should be, based on millions of past sentences it has read.

HMM relies on two important probabilities:

1. **Transition Probability:** 
   What is the mathematical chance that one grammatical tag will follow another?
   *Example:* What is the probability that a Noun comes after an Adjective? Since "Big Dog" is common, this probability is very high. What is the probability that a Verb comes after an Adjective? Very low.

2. **Emission Probability:** 
   What is the mathematical chance that a specific part-of-speech tag will produce a specific word?
   *Example:* Out of all the Verbs in the world, what is the probability that the Verb is exactly the word "run"?

**How the model chooses the answer:**
For an entire sentence, the computer multiplies these probabilities together across multiple different tag combinations. The combination that gives the **highest final mathematical score** is chosen as the final answer. It is fast, automated, and much cheaper than writing human rules.

A <Highlight>**Context-Free Grammar (CFG)**</Highlight> is a set of logical rules used to build grammatically correct sentences. Think of it like a formula for producing a proper English sentence.

CFG helps machines understand the structure of a sentence (which part is the subject, the verb, etc).

It consists of 4 main parts:
1. **Terminals (T):** The actual, final words in the dictionary (e.g., *book, flight, man, the*).
2. **Non-terminals (N):** The grammatical categories or placeholders (e.g., Noun, Verb, NounPhrase).
3. **Start symbol (S):** The starting point, usually an entire Sentence.
4. **Rules (R):** The instructions that tell how a Non-terminal converts into Terminals.

**Example of CFG Rules:**
- `Sentence → NounPhrase + VerbPhrase`
- `NounPhrase → Determiner + Noun`
- `VerbPhrase → Verb + NounPhrase`
- `Determiner → "the" | "a"`
- `Verb → "reads" | "books"`
- `Noun → "man" | "book"`

**Example Tree Structure:**
Using the rules above, the sentence "the man reads a book" can be mapped accurately:

Whenever text goes into an NLP system, it is processed deeply through five sequential stages:

1. **Morphological Analysis (Word Level):**
   The computer breaks large words down into their smallest meaningful parts (morphemes).
   *Example:* `Truthfulness` is split into `Truth` + `ful` + `ness`.

2. **Syntactic Analysis (Grammar Level):**
   The computer checks if the sentence follows strict language grammar rules using sentence parse trees. It checks if the verbs and nouns are correctly placed.

3. **Semantic Analysis (Meaning Level):**
   The computer figures out the literal dictionary meaning of the sentence and handles words that have multiple meanings.
   *Example:* Decoding if "Apple" means the fruit or the technology company.

4. **Discourse Analysis (Sentence Level):**
   The computer connects the current sentence to the previous sentences to figure out pronouns.
   *Example:* If sentence 1 is "Rahul bought a car" and sentence 2 is "He drove it", Discourse analysis connects "He" to "Rahul".

5. **Pragmatic Analysis (Context Level):**
   The computer uncovers the hidden intention of the sentence based on human situational context.
   *Example:* If a user says "Can you pass the salt?", they aren't asking if you possess the physical capability to lift salt. They are requesting you to give it to them.

Resolving ambiguity is critical because if NLP picks the wrong meaning, the entire output to the user will be completely incorrect. NLP systems use four main methods to resolve these massive ambiguity problems:

#### 1. Resolving Syntactic Ambiguity using POS Tagging
When a word like `book` or `race` can be multiple things, NLP uses **Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging**. It looks closely at the words immediately before and after the ambiguous word. 
*Example:* In the sentence "I will book the flight", the presence of "will" before "book" triggers a grammatical rule that categorizes "book" as an Action/Verb, destroying the incorrect interpretation that it is a paper object.

#### 2. Resolving Lexical/Semantic Ambiguity using Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD)
When a word is definitively a Noun, but has two meanings (like `bank` being a river bank vs `bank` being a financial building), NLP uses **WSD**. 
WSD scans the rest of the paragraph for context clues. If words like "money", "deposit", and "loan" are in the same paragraph, it mathematical calculates that "financial building" is the correct definition.

#### 3. Resolving Parsing Ambiguity using Probabilistic parsing
When a whole sentence can be interpreted in two ways (like the telescope example), the NLP system uses **Probabilistic Parsing**. It builds a syntax tree for BOTH possible meanings. Then, it checks an massive database of billions of real-world sentences to calculate which meaning happens more often in real life. It picks the tree with the highest probability.

#### 4. Resolving Pragmatic Ambiguity using Context Tracking
To understand hidden intentions (like sarcasm, or answering "Can you pass the salt?"), the computer must use **Pragmatics**. It tracks the history of the conversation and uses a "Real-World Knowledge Base" to understand human situational context, realizing it is a request for an object rather than a yes-or-no question.

A **Regular Expression (RE)** is a <Highlight>**simple and formal language used to match text patterns**</Highlight> inside strings. Just like how we use a search box to find a file, NLP uses regular expressions to find specific words or letters in a large amount of text.

They are very important in Natural Language Processing for tasks like searching text, cleaning data, and checking if a word fits a certain grammatical rule.

**Basic rule operators and symbols:**

| Operator | What it does | Example | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `[A-Z]` | Finds any capital letter | `[Ww]oodchuck` | Matches `Woodchuck` or `woodchuck` |
| `[^A-Z]` | Negation (Finds anything EXCEPT capital letters) | `[^A-Z]at` | Matches `cat`, `bat`, but NOT `Cat` |
| `[e^]` | Matches either the letter 'e' or the character '^' literally (since '^' is not at the start) | `m[e^]t` | Matches `met` or `m^t` |
| `?` | Optional character (0 or 1 time) | `colou?r` | Matches `color` or `colour` |
| `.` | Wildcard (Matches any single character) | `beg.n` | Matches `began`, `begin`, `begun` |
| `*` | Zero or more times | `ab*` | Matches `a`, `ab`, `abb`, `abbb` |
| `+` | One or more times | `ab+` | Matches `ab`, `abb`, `abbb` (but NOT just `a`) |
| `\d` | Finds any digit | `\d\d` | Matches `12`, `99` |

**Applications:**
Regular expressions are widely used for finding emails in a text, cleaning up messy data, and creating rules for morphological parsers.

A <Highlight>**language model**</Highlight> goes through a text to calculate the probability of a word sequence. It answers the question: *"What is the most likely next word after this sentence?"*

An **N-gram model** is a simple version of this. Instead of looking at the entire paragraph, it looks at only a fixed number of previous words (called the window). This helps the computer run much faster.

**Different Types of N-grams:**

1. **Unigram (N=1):** The model only looks at 1 word at a time. It ignores all previous words.  
   *Example sentence "I love coding":* `[I]`, `[love]`, `[coding]`
2. **Bigram (N=2):** The model looks at the current word and 1 previous word.  
   *Example:* `[I love]`, `[love coding]`
3. **Trigram (N=3):** The model looks at 2 previous words.  
   *Example:* `[I love coding]`

**How it works (The Formula):**
To guess the next word in a Bigram model, it uses a simple probability formula based on how many times it saw the two words together in the past:

$$
P(word_2 \mid word_1) = \frac{\text{Count}(word_1, word_2)}{\text{Count}(word_1)}
$$

**Limitations & Solutions:**
- **The Zero Probability Problem:** If an N-gram model has never seen a specific pair of words before (like "purple alien"), it will give a 0% probability, breaking the whole calculation.
- **Solution:** We use **Smoothing** (like Laplace Add-1 smoothing) which gives a tiny bit of probability (like 0.001) to unseen words so the system doesn't crash.

#### 1. Natural Language Understanding (NLU)
This is the **"Reading and Listening"** part of NLP. It focuses on taking an input from a human (text or speech) and mapping it into a meaning that the computer understands. 
- It breaks down the sentence to find the emotion, the grammar, and the hidden context.
- *Goal:* Successfully understand the full meaning of what a human just said.

This is the **"Writing and Speaking"** part of NLP. Once the computer has processed a thought in its own machine code, it must convert it back into an English sentence to present to the user.
- It involves deep planning on *what to say* and syntactic generation on *how to build the grammar of the sentence properly*.
- *Goal:* Successfully produce a human-sounding response.

<Highlight>**Ambiguity**</Highlight> happens when a sentence or a word has more than one meaning. It is one of the hardest challenges to solve in NLP.

There are five major types of ambiguity:

1. **Lexical / Morphological Ambiguity:**
   When a single word has more than one meaning in the dictionary.
   *Example:* The word "bat" can mean a flying bird/mammal, or it can mean wooden sports equipment used in cricket.

2. **Phonetic Ambiguity:**
   When two words have completely different spellings and completely different meanings, but sound exactly the same when spoken out loud.
   *Example:* "write", "right", and "rite" are all pronounced the exact same way.

3. **Semantic Ambiguity:**
   When the grammar of a sentence is fully correct, but the meaning gives two different interpretations.
   *Example:* "The chicken is ready to eat." 
   Does this mean the animal is hungry and wants food? Or does this mean the food is cooked and ready on the table?

4. **Syntactic (Parsing) Ambiguity:**
   When a sentence can be parsed in multiple grammatical ways.
   *Example:* "I saw a girl with a telescope."
   Did I use a telescope to observe a girl from far away? Or did I look at a girl who was holding a telescope? 

5. **Pragmatic Ambiguity:**
   When the user's intentions are clear but only if you have contextual knowledge of the situation. Without knowing the context, the computer is confused.

**Morphology** is the deep study of how words are formed, created, and structured. 

The smallest foundational unit behind morphology is called a **Morpheme**. A morpheme is the absolute smallest piece of a word that still carries its own meaning. Once you break a morpheme down any further, it's just random letters that mean nothing.

For example, look at the word: **`unhappiness`**
It has three morphemes:
- `un` (prefix meaning 'not')
- `happy` (the base root meaning joy)
- `ness` (suffix meaning 'a state of')

**Morphological Analysis** is the process where a computer actively takes large words and chops them down into their base morphemes so the computer can understand exactly what the root meaning of the word is. 
*Example:* Chopping `walking` into `walk` + `ing`.

Both Stemming and Lemmatization are techniques used in NLP to chop the ends off of words to reduce them back to their base, root form. However, they do it in very different ways.

| Stemming | Lemmatization |
|----------|---------------|
| **No Dictionary:** Stemming blindly chops endings off using basic math rules without checking a dictionary. | **Uses Dictionary:** Lemmatization carefully checks a language dictionary and grammar context. |
| **Low Accuracy:** Because it blindly cuts, the output word is sometimes not a real English word. | **High Accuracy:** It always outputs a 100% valid, real English word. |
| **Very Fast:** It is mathematically very simple and fast to run. | **Very Slow:** Because it searches a massive dictionary, it takes much longer to process. |
| **Example 1:** `Studying` gets chopped poorly into `Studi` (Not a real word). | **Example 1:** `Studying` safely converts into `Study` (Real word). |
| **Example 2:** `Happiness` becomes `Happi`. | **Example 2:** `Mice` safely translates perfectly back to `Mouse`. |

The **Porter Stemmer** is the most famous and widely used algorithm to perform "Stemming" on text. 

Because stemming doesn't use a dictionary, the Porter Stemmer created a massive checklist of **suffix-stripping rules** to systematically chop the ends off words.

It classifies words by their sequences of vowels and consonants. Based on how many sequences there are, it runs the word through a 5-step process:

- **Step 1:** It removes obvious plural signs and simple past tense endings (e.g. `caresses` becomes `caress`, `ponies` becomes `poni`).
- **Step 2 to 4:** It looks deeper for complex suffixes like `-ation` and reduces them (e.g. `generalization` gets brutally chopped down to `generalize`).
- **Step 5:** It performs final cleanup, removing extra `e`s or `l`s at the end of the newly chopped word.

Because of this brutal set of rules, the Porter algorithm is incredibly fast, but predictably creates non-dictionary words as its final output.
